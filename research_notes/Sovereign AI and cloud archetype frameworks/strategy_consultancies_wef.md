## 1. What exactly are the archetypes (verbatim names) and how is each defined?

### Takeaway
Four distinct published taxonomies exist and they do not map one-to-one: WEF/Bain has **five** country archetypes (Global AI value chain leaders, Ecosystem builders, Selective players, Adoption accelerators, Emerging collaborators) plus five numbered pathways that all converge on "ecosystem builder"; McKinsey has **two** taxonomies (a four-class "Sovereign AI nation archetypes" scheme A/B1/B2/C in its June 2026 deck, and a set of "ecosystem archetypes" in its March 2026 article whose exact labels I could only get via search summaries); BCG has **six** readiness/exposure archetypes (2024) that are not sovereignty archetypes at all, plus a superpower/middle-power split and, in 2026, an explicit "resilience not sovereignty" four-lever model. Bain alone (Technology Report 2025) publishes no named archetypes.

### Cited Findings

#### 1A. WEF with Bain & Company — "Rethinking AI Sovereignty: Pathways to Competitiveness through Strategic Investments" (white paper, January 2026) (primary, read in full)
- Publication: white paper dated "JANUARY 2026", published by the WEF AI Global Alliance "In collaboration with Bain & Company"; foreword by Florian Mueller (Senior Partner and Head, AI, Insights & Solutions for EMEA, Bain) and Cathy Li (Head, Centre for AI Excellence, WEF). Lead authors listed: Maria Basso (WEF, Head, AI Applications and Impact) and Mariana Justo Pereira (Bain consultant; Project Fellow, AI Global Alliance); other named contributors include Tarik Fayad (WEF MENA Lead, AI Strategic Integration), Aron Philipp, Shreya Sahay, Philipp Sautner (Bain), Francesca Zanolla (WEF). — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf). Search results give the release day as 20 January 2026 (search summary) — [WEF landing page](https://www.weforum.org/publications/rethinking-ai-sovereignty/).
- It builds on the Forum's January 2025 white paper "Blueprint for Intelligent Economies: AI Competitiveness through Regional Collaboration" and "will be followed by targeted publications detailing how to build different elements of sovereign AI ecosystems". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- Definition of AI sovereignty (verbatim): "the ability of economies to shape, deploy and govern AI ecosystems in accordance with their own values, whilst ensuring strategic and operational control, flexibility and, ultimately, resilience through a combination of localized investment and trusted international collaboration." — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- **The five archetypes, verbatim names (Figure 5/6, Boxes 1–5):** (1) **"Global AI value chain leaders"**, (2) **"Ecosystem builders"**, (3) **"Selective players"**, (4) **"Adoption accelerators"**, (5) **"Emerging collaborators"**. The paper says the analysis "revealed five major recurring patterns, grouped into archetypes ranging from global AI value chain leaders to emerging collaborators". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- **Global AI value chain leaders** — Overview: "End-to-end investors across the AI value chain, with world-class capabilities in every element and enabler, pursuing dominance by maintaining or expanding their leadership positions in AI technology and innovation". Typical profile: "Global economic leaders with extensive research and financial capacity". Investments "of unique global scale across all AI value chain elements, including hardware"; "Unique capacity to fund and develop foundation models with global scale (e.g. frontier LLMs)". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- **Ecosystem builders** — Overview: "Orchestrators pursuing balanced, government-driven investment strategies, building strong local AI ecosystems and regional influence, and increasingly joining international alliances and partnerships". Typical profile: "Smaller advanced economies leveraging agile governance and strong access to capital and resources". Features: "Strong, balanced investments across most AI value chain elements (except hardware)"; "Dense, efficient AI infrastructure serving as regional hubs, with national champions playing key roles (e.g. by partnering with hyperscalers and trusted vendors)"; "Investments in regionally focused or sovereign foundation models (e.g. SEA-LION) with limited global scale"; "Focus on AI applications in government and priority sectors"; "Strong government-led initiatives targeting all enablers". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- **Selective players** — Overview: "Targeted players with moderate adoption and selected strengths in the AI ecosystem, investing in sector-specific data and applications". Typical profile: "Established industrial economies leveraging strong research and manufacturing bases". Features: "Orientation towards regionally focused and open foundation models (e.g. Mistral) aligned with ethical and regulatory standards"; "Selective investments in data and applications, mainly for manufacturing, mobility and government services"; "Reliance on international partnerships for other elements, particularly hardware and compute, with focus on sovereign facilities"; "Mature coverage of select enablers such as R&D and education, though some gaps persist (e.g. capital access)"; "Predominantly government-led investments supported by public research institutions and industrial partnerships". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- **Adoption accelerators** — Overview: "Rapid commercial deployers with wide AI adoption that focus on driving application development, scaling use cases and demand first, then selectively backfilling AI infrastructure through partnerships". Typical profile: "Large emerging economies driven by a fast-growing digital sector and foreign investment". Features: "Continued reliance on external partners for hardware and foundation models due to limited local capacity"; "Domestic AI infrastructure build-out through public-private partnerships"; "Early-stage investment in enabling technology, such as digital public infrastructure". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- **Emerging collaborators** — Overview: "Early-stage actors with nascent, fragmented AI capabilities still in the stage of exploring the AI landscape and potential partnerships". Typical profile: "Emerging economies with modest research and financial capacity, in some cases with a large share of GDP coming from routine manufacturing or service activities facing AI-driven automation". Features: "Reliance on international cooperation and donor-backed programmes"; "AI Infrastructure capacity generally in planning or early construction phases with very limited access to hardware"; "Reliance on open or partner foundation models through academic or regional collaborations"; "Leveraging public-private partnerships and development finance". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- **The five pathways, verbatim titles (all end at "ecosystem builders"; none targets "global AI value chain leaders"):** "Pathway 1: From selective players to ecosystem builders"; "Pathway 2: From adoption accelerators to ecosystem builders"; "Pathway 3: From emerging collaborators to ecosystem builders via adoption accelerators"; "Pathway 4: From emerging collaborators to ecosystem builders via selective players"; "Pathway 5: From emerging collaborators to ecosystem builders". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- Caveat stated in the paper: archetypes "are not rankings or sequential steps to be followed"; "Archetypes are not fixed categories"; "only very few economies can become global AI value chain leaders ... Most economies will instead achieve better outcomes by becoming effective ecosystem builders". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)

#### 1B. WEF with Bain — follow-up "AI Infrastructure in the Age of Sovereignty: Requirements, Strategies and a Trusted Framework for Digital Embassies" (white paper, May 2026) (primary, partially read)
- Dated "MAY 2026", AI Global Alliance "In collaboration with Bain & Company"; foreword again includes Florian Mueller (Bain); explicitly positioned as the third in the series after "Blueprint for Intelligent Economies" and "Rethinking AI Sovereignty". — [WEF-DE](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_AI_Infrastructure_in_the_Age_of_Sovereignty_Requirements_Strategies_and_a_Trusted_Framework_for_Digital_Embassies_2026.pdf). Release date reported as 11 May 2026 (search summary) — [ComplexDiscovery](https://complexdiscovery.com/digital-embassies-get-a-global-rulebook-in-wef-and-bain-white-paper/).
- It does not add country archetypes; it defines an **"AI sovereignty spectrum"** of interdependence with **two "reference strategies"**: **"trusted international partnerships"** (higher interdependency) and **"extensive domestic ownership"** (lower interdependency). "these reference strategies are not binary choices. In practice, most economies blend elements of both". — [WEF-DE](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_AI_Infrastructure_in_the_Age_of_Sovereignty_Requirements_Strategies_and_a_Trusted_Framework_for_Digital_Embassies_2026.pdf)
- Trusted-partnership arrangements named: "Digital embassies: Sovereign capacity is delivered through secure spaces; an economy's own laws govern its data, even when hosted abroad" and "Pooled, multi-economy capacity" (example: EuroHPC Joint Undertaking). "The trusted international partnerships strategy requires local ownership of connectivity, but not compute or data storage". — [WEF-DE](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_AI_Infrastructure_in_the_Age_of_Sovereignty_Requirements_Strategies_and_a_Trusted_Framework_for_Digital_Embassies_2026.pdf)
- "Only a small number of economies – primarily China and the US – can come close to achieving extensive domestic ownership"; Alibaba Cloud, Huawei Cloud and Tencent Cloud "jointly hold roughly 70% of cloud infrastructure market share" in China. — [WEF-DE](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_AI_Infrastructure_in_the_Age_of_Sovereignty_Requirements_Strategies_and_a_Trusted_Framework_for_Digital_Embassies_2026.pdf)
- "Global framework for innovative and trusted digital embassies": five trust dimensions — "Political commitment", "Legal basis and scope of agreement", "Data management", "Technical policies and safeguards", "Operational rules" (centred on "Mutual trust"). — [WEF-DE](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_AI_Infrastructure_in_the_Age_of_Sovereignty_Requirements_Strategies_and_a_Trusted_Framework_for_Digital_Embassies_2026.pdf)
- Digital-embassy legal models cited: treaty-based (Estonia–Luxembourg, 2017 bilateral agreement); host-statute (Bahrain law letting foreign jurisdictions apply their own laws to designated cloud environments); and "Saudi Arabia's draft Global AI Hub Law", which "outlines three types of legally defined hubs": "Private hubs" (dedicated environments exclusively under guest-country law), "Extended hubs" (operator/users may host workloads under guest-country law) and "Virtual hubs" (shared environments where foreign workloads operate under foreign-state legal authority). — [WEF-DE](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_AI_Infrastructure_in_the_Age_of_Sovereignty_Requirements_Strategies_and_a_Trusted_Framework_for_Digital_Embassies_2026.pdf)
- A WEF Davos session "Digital Embassies for Sovereign AI" (January 2026) announced the framework effort; G42 launched its own "Digital Embassies" framework at Davos (search summary). — [ComplexDiscovery](https://complexdiscovery.com/digital-embassies-get-a-global-rulebook-in-wef-and-bain-white-paper/); [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/business/markets/g42-unveils-digital-embassies-to-let-nations-deploy-ai-with-full-control-1.500414383)
- A WEF Stories article on shared infrastructure for AI sovereignty exists at https://www.weforum.org/stories/artificial-intelligence/shared-infrastructure-ai-sovereignty/ but returned 403; content not verified (see Gaps).

#### 1C. McKinsey — "Sovereign AI nation archetypes" (deck, 18 June 2026) (primary, read in full)
- Source is a McKinsey & Company-branded deck, "European Sovereign AI – aspiration or reality?", presented at the Cambridge Risk Symposium on 18 June 2026 and hosted publicly by Cambridge Judge Business School under the file name "Ruben-Schaubroeck-Sovereign-AI.pdf" (Schaubroeck is also a named co-author of the March 2026 McKinsey article per the UDLAP citation). The slides carry a "CONFIDENTIAL AND PROPRIETARY" footer although publicly posted. — [MCK-DECK](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Ruben-Schaubroeck-Sovereign-AI.pdf)
- **"Sovereign AI nation archetypes" (verbatim, slide "only a handful of countries have AI compute today – most are consumer nations"):**
  - **A — "AI Consumers & Challengers in AI"** "(e.g. Africa, Central Asia, Latin America, smaller Southeast Asian economies)"
  - **B1 — "Targeted Sovereign Players – producers for home use"** "(e.g. EU esp. France, UK, Italy, Japan, India)"
  - **B2 — "AI hubs – producers for export use"** "(e.g. KSA, Qatar, UAE, Singapore)"
  - **C — "Fully Sovereign Builders – producers and exporters of AI"** "(e.g. US, China)"
  — [MCK-DECK](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Ruben-Schaubroeck-Sovereign-AI.pdf)
- On the national-strategy "decision points" slide the same four are named as design options for "Target national archetype": "Fully sovereign builder", "Targeted sovereign player", "AI Hub & platform provider", "Consumer nation". — [MCK-DECK](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Ruben-Schaubroeck-Sovereign-AI.pdf)
- Supporting stat on the archetype slide: "Only 30 countries have in-country training or inference capability" (2025; source cited as A16Z, Oxford Internet Institute). — [MCK-DECK](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Ruben-Schaubroeck-Sovereign-AI.pdf)
- **Deployment-level tiers (unit = cloud/AI offering, not country):** "Tier 1: Fully sovereign" (Disconnected AI services; On-Prem; Fully local AI cloud services), "Tier 2: Partially sovereign" (Hybrid stack AI services; Local entity AI services; Localized data / ops services — examples plotted include Google Sovereign Controls / GDC, Cloud@Customer, Sovereign zone, Microsoft Cloud for Sovereignty, Outposts), "Tier 3: Public stack" (Public AI cloud with sovereign controls; Basic public AI cloud — e.g. EKS with Thales, EU Regions, EU Data Boundary). Slide title: "Sovereign AI comes in many shades, allowing local players to challenge global tech incumbents". — [MCK-DECK](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Ruben-Schaubroeck-Sovereign-AI.pdf)
- **Stakeholder "possible plays" (unit = organisation):** for "Takers" (enterprise & public-sector CxOs): "Full-stack sovereign builder" and "Sovereign AI services consumer"; for "Makers" (providers "e.g., NeoClouds, TelCos, Software companies, SIs"): "Market entry", "Product/market fit and offering", "GtM acceleration"; for Government: "Stack builder" and "Ecosystem orchestrator"; for investors ("PE/VC, SWF"): "Strategic champion investor" ("How do I choose which local AI player to invest in, and how do I support scale-up?"). — [MCK-DECK](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Ruben-Schaubroeck-Sovereign-AI.pdf)

#### 1E. BCG — AI Maturity Matrix (Nov 2024; updated Oct 2026) (2024 report: primary, read in full)

  - **"Rising contenders"** (sub-group of "AI contenders") — "Economies with relatively low exposure to AI despite high readiness for its adoption"

#### 1G. Bain (own-brand) — Technology Report 2025 chapter "Sovereign Tech, Fragmented World"
- Published 23 September 2025; authors Anne Hoecker, Karen Harris, Jonathan Frick, Ravi Vijayaraghavan. No named archetypes. Regional strategy contrasts instead: China "pursues end-to-end control of its AI stack"; Europe prioritises regulatory alignment and data localisation; the Middle East seeks participation in the global AI ecosystem; some countries will build AI capacity while others "buy from the cheapest available source" (primary, via fetch summary — wording of the three regional postures is the fetch tool's paraphrase). — [BAIN-TR25](https://www.bain.com/insights/sovereign-tech-fragmented-world-technology-report-2025/)
- Bain states sovereign AI is now treated as a strategic asset "on par with economic and military strength" and that full technological self-reliance is largely infeasible for most countries. — [BAIN-TR25](https://www.bain.com/insights/sovereign-tech-fragmented-world-technology-report-2025/)

### Inferences
- Bain's archetype thinking is published through the WEF papers (Jan and May 2026), not under its own brand; the Technology Report 2025 chapter is the conceptual precursor ("full self-reliance infeasible" → "strategic interdependence").

## 2. What classification logic is used (axes, scoring, value-chain coverage, unit of analysis)?

### Takeaway
WEF/Bain classifies economies on two axes — breadth of coverage of six AI value-chain elements (y) and maturity of enablers (x) — using presence/scale thresholds; McKinsey classifies nations by whether they consume, produce for home use, produce for export, or do all (anchored in a 4-control-dimension × 3-layer sovereignty matrix and an eight-layer ecosystem stack); BCG's matrix is a readiness (ASPIRE, 33 indicators) × exposure grid that measures preparedness for AI disruption rather than sovereignty.

## 4. What does each framework recommend per archetype — and what does it say about national champions, ownership models, telecom operators, sovereign wealth funds, PPPs?

### Takeaway
All four houses converge on "partnership, not ownership": WEF/Bain ("strategic interdependence"), McKinsey (shared ecosystems, named partnership models, sequencing demand before infrastructure), BCG ("resilience", "use before ownership"). National champions are endorsed mainly for hub/ecosystem-builder and pioneer archetypes; PPPs, blended finance and demand aggregation are the default prescription for everyone else. Telcos are treated as national-champion infrastructure partners (WEF: Singtel) and as provider-side "makers" (McKinsey), but none of the frameworks has a telco-specific archetype.

### Cited Findings

**WEF / Bain (Jan 2026) — per pathway**
- Pathway 1 (selective players → ecosystem builders): "strengthen the funding environment (e.g. through public-private mechanisms) and incentivize early-stage innovation"; focus on elements "that complement and add value vs. global AI value chain leaders, rather than attempting to replicate them, while negotiating access to complementary capabilities"; sector-specific roadmaps (health, energy); cooperate "with both local players and global hyperscalers"; capital-market depth and risk-sharing mechanisms. Starting point includes "a strong R&D base often linked with industrial champions". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- Pathway 2 (adoption accelerators → ecosystem builders): "use blended financing models to expand compute and cloud capacity"; coordinated industrial policy linking digital infrastructure with R&D and workforce; "National data utilities and open data platforms"; IP legislation, capital markets, risk-sharing; applied research, accelerators, pilots. — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- Pathway 3 (emerging collaborators → via adoption accelerators): "Rather than pursuing full sovereign AI infrastructure, these economies can use PPPs, pay-as-you-go pricing models and concessional finance to expand access"; invest in connectivity; curated data; "partnering on open-source or allied foundation models"; affordable compute for start-ups; university partnerships with regional AI hubs; SME lighthouse projects. — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- Pathway 4 (emerging collaborators → via selective players; for economies "with high availability of capital, land and energy, strategic location and regional connectivity advantages"): "invest in excess AI infrastructure capacity and serve both domestic and neighbouring markets as regional compute hubs"; "trusted technology sourcing, such as advanced chips, and long-term offtake arrangements"; "host external foundation models through partnerships"; "investment frameworks and incentives for PPPs"; strengthen energy, land-use and permitting policy; regulatory certainty and data governance to "secure foreign direct investment". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- Pathway 5 (emerging collaborators → directly to ecosystem builders; "strong energy base, high land availability ... strategic location with advanced data connectivity"): "simultaneously developing data, applications and AI infrastructure"; strategic partnerships for local computing infrastructure; incentivise "local data centres, national data platforms and open-sourced or allied foundation models"; become "a regional AI hub, where global technologies integrate with domestic strengths". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- Five policy priorities (Chapter 4): (1) "Support the development of clear local strategies that play to strengths"; (2) "Help reframe AI sovereignty as strategic interdependence"; (3) "Enable targeted investments to strengthen the local AI ecosystem"; (4) "Promote interoperable AI infrastructure"; (5) "Facilitate the use of data and applications to encourage responsible adoption". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- Key lines: "Full AI sovereignty is not feasible for economies"; "Partnership, not ownership, is a key path forward"; "Policy-makers should help promote operational control, AI ecosystem health and flexibility over ownership, encouraging action now while retaining the option to localize later"; "Full AI sovereignty is out of reach. Lasting advantages are not." — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- National champions / ownership / SWF / PPP: ecosystem builders have "national champions playing key roles (e.g. by partnering with hyperscalers and trusted vendors)" (Singtel, STT GDC, Keppel); "national cloud providers such as HUMAIN (Saudi Arabia), G42 (United Arab Emirates), Outscale (France) and STACKIT (Germany)"; "Public investment has become a critical driver ... including HUMAIN, which is backed by the Saudi Public Investment Fund (PIF). Governments are increasingly co-investing in large-scale data centre and compute projects through PPPs"; "Since 2019, investment priorities have shifted from ownership to partnership-driven models"; AI infrastructure "increasingly viewed as a strategic public asset"; MGX appears in the Stargate fund-flow figure. — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)

- Telecom operators: no telco-specific recommendation; telcos appear only via Singtel as a Singapore national champion and "Enabling technology (especially connectivity)" as an enabler. — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)

**McKinsey**
- Named partnership models (March 2026 article, via repost): "Standardized sovereign zones, demand aggregation frameworks, joint operating models, data and model consortiums, and blended finance". Features of effective ecosystems: demand-led anchors, in-country AI infrastructure, a sovereignty baseline and reference architecture, trusted governance and standards, strong data ecosystems with modular model strategies, aligned capital structures, local talent development. Three overlapping waves: (1) establish a baseline with prioritised sovereign workloads and lighthouse use cases; (2) scale shared infrastructure and data ecosystems; (3) deepen specialisation to create exportable capabilities. Main failure mode: mis-sequencing (investing heavily before demand, governance and talent are ready). Key actors: governments, technology providers, enterprises, investors. (search summary) — [MCK-UDLAP](https://observatorioglobal.udlap.mx/sovereign-ai-building-ecosystems-for-strategic-resilience-and-impact/)

## 5. Known critiques or limitations of each framework

### Takeaway
I found little formal third-party critique of these specific frameworks; the main limitations are structural and partly self-acknowledged: WEF/Bain's assignments are illustrative (one or two flags per archetype, thresholds in an endnote) and the paper's contributors include many infrastructure vendors and Gulf investors; McKinsey's archetypes are asserted without a scoring model and sit alongside a TAM estimate that sizes its own advisory market; BCG's matrix measures readiness and exposure rather than sovereignty, and BCG itself now argues stack-based sovereignty is an "illusion".

### Cited Findings
- WEF/Bain self-acknowledged limits: archetypes "are not rankings or sequential steps"; "each economy's context and trajectory are different"; pathways "represent desirable, medium-term options ... Other routes may exist"; the disclaimer says results "do not necessarily represent the views of the World Economic Forum, nor the entirety of its Members, Partners or other stakeholders". — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- WEF/Bain's own critique of the field: "'AI sovereignty' has been conflated with 'AI infrastructure' and data centres." — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- Stakeholder composition: acknowledged contributors to the WEF paper include executives from Mubadala Investment Company, G42, Amazon Web Services, CoreWeave, Palantir, Cloudflare, Cisco and IBM, plus C4IR Saudi Arabia and C4IR Rwanda. — [WEF-RAS](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)
- Third-party critique of the digital-embassy/shared-access agenda: Sociable questioned whether shared access would mainly benefit wealthier governments, arguing the plan is to build data centres in host countries that already have the money and resources (search summary; article not read). — [Sociable](https://sociable.co/?p=100537)
- A Substack essay titled "Sovereign AI is a Resource Curse in the Making" surfaced alongside the WEF paper in search results; content not read, so its argument is not characterised here. — [realpolitik Substack](https://realpol.substack.com/p/sovereign-ai-is-a-resource-curse)
- McKinsey: the sovereign AI TAM is a demand estimate that "differs from actual spend captured by sovereign AI solutions" and rests on a "McKinsey Proprietary Sovereign AI Model"; the nation archetypes are given with "e.g." examples only. — [MCK-DECK](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Ruben-Schaubroeck-Sovereign-AI.pdf)
- McKinsey reportedly notes there is no official definition of sovereign AI (search summary). — [MCK-ART](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/sovereign-ai-building-ecosystems-for-strategic-resilience-and-impact)

- BCG's internal counter-position (Mar 2026): single-layer "sovereignty" plays are "exceptionally resource intensive"; stack-based sovereignty "implicitly relies on the assumption that today's compute-intensive, LLM-based AI paradigm will continue to dominate", risking "massive sunk costs into stack-based moonshots"; early sovereign cloud schemes such as GAIA-X "became mired in complex governance committees and branding exercises, producing merely symbolic frameworks rather than usable capacity". — [BHI-RES](https://web-assets.bcg.com/pdf-src/prod-live/ai-sovereignty-is-an-illusion-resilience-is-real.pdf)
- WEF's May 2026 paper flags systemic risk in the infrastructure build-out itself: "Growing reliance on increasingly complex financing structures and greater use of debt mean that disruptions or underperformance can have spillover effects across the wider financial system." — [WEF-DE](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_AI_Infrastructure_in_the_Age_of_Sovereignty_Requirements_Strategies_and_a_Trusted_Framework_for_Digital_Embassies_2026.pdf)

### Inferences
- Vendor/advisory interest: all three consultancies advise governments, SWFs and national champions on exactly these programmes, and the WEF papers are co-produced with hyperscalers, neoclouds and Gulf investors among the contributors; the shared conclusion ("partner with trusted global providers rather than own the stack") aligns with those contributors' commercial interests. This is my inference from authorship/acknowledgement lists, not a critique found in a published source.
- Conflating readiness with strategy: BCG's matrix ranks readiness/exposure and is often cited as if it were a sovereignty ranking; e.g. Saudi Arabia and the UAE are only "rising contenders" (2024) on readiness while being "AI hubs" (McKinsey) / "ecosystem builder" (UAE, WEF) on strategy. Report writers should not merge these scales.
- Teleology: every WEF pathway ends at "ecosystem builder" (the Singapore/UAE model), which implicitly treats small, capital-rich, government-orchestrated hubs as the universal target and gives no pathway for large industrial economies other than Pathway 1.
- Thin evidence base for assignments: WEF shows eight flags for five archetypes; McKinsey's A/B1/B2/C uses regional "e.g." lists; neither publishes a full country table, so placements of unlisted countries (Saudi Arabia in WEF; Morocco, Germany, Korea in McKinsey) are extrapolations.
- Instability over time: Korea moved from BCG "steady contender" (2024) to "AI Pioneer" (2026) in two years, and India is described by WEF as mid-transition between archetypes — archetype labels date quickly.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed or think-tank critique that addresses the WEF/Bain five-archetype model, McKinsey's nation archetypes, or BCG's matrix by name; the Sociable and Substack pieces were surfaced but not read in full.
- No independent validation of McKinsey's $500–600 billion sovereign AI TAM was found.
