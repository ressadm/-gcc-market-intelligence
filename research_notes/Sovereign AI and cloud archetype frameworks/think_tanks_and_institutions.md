## 1. What exactly are the categories/archetypes (verbatim) and definitions?

### Takeaway
Across ~20 institutional frameworks there is no shared country typology: think tanks classify *postures per stack layer* (TBI "Control, Steer, Depend"), *scenarios* (Carnegie's four futures), *leverage/asset types* (Carnegie's six middle-power types, GMF's asset classes, Chatham House's four pathways), *infrastructure geography* (Oxford's "Compute North / Compute South / Compute Desert"), *capability quadrants* (UNCTAD "Leaders / Creators / Practitioners / Laggards"; World Bank low/medium/high readiness), or *assurance grades for providers* (EU SEAL-0 to SEAL-4). Almost none uses consultancy-style "full-stack builder" archetypes; the dominant shared concept is "managed interdependence" (Carnegie, Brookings, GMF, TBI, Stanford HAI all use that phrase or a near-synonym).

### Cited Findings

#### 1.1 Carnegie Endowment

**(a) "Early Lessons in the Pursuit of Sovereign AI" — Shreya Joshi, 17 June 2026 (Digital Democracy Network series, Democracy, Conflict and Governance Program).**
- Core thesis, verbatim: "attaining complete sovereignty is unrealistic for most nations"; the alternative offered is "strategic autonomy" or "managed interdependence" as a third way between dependence on the US or China and full independence. — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai)
- The essay does NOT assign formal archetype labels to India and the UAE; it is a two-case comparison. India is described as "a lower-middle-income democracy"; the UAE as "a small, capital-rich Gulf monarchy". Any "India archetype vs UAE archetype" labelling is the reader's shorthand, not Carnegie's. — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai)
- Definition of sovereign AI: a nation's ability to "design, develop, and govern AI systems" using domestic infrastructure, nationally held data and a homegrown talent base; treated as a spectrum across the stack (semiconductors, cloud, data centres, foundation models, digital infrastructure), not a binary. — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai)
- "Managed interdependence" as described: a hybrid approach in which countries build sovereign capacity incrementally in specific sectors while partnering with like-minded countries to reduce risks such as vendor lock-in and asymmetric bargaining power. — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai)
- India model (as described): national foundation models (Sarvam AI for 22 languages, BharatGen) plus public compute (IndiaAI Mission, launched 2024); the IndiaAI compute facility had 38,000 GPUs "all supplied by American firms" as of early 2026; sovereign AI sector raised over $5.5 billion across 1,700 firms; strong data-localisation governance; results of semiconductor/data-centre funding "limited so far". — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai)
- UAE model (as described): reportedly $148 billion spent on AI at home and abroad since start of 2024, backed by sovereign wealth funds; G42 ("a private AI company chaired by the UAE's national security adviser", Sheikh Tahnoun bin Zayed) as main vehicle; open-weight models Falcon, Jais, K2 Think; leverage via hosting American supercomputers, investing in American firms, and "negotiated access, co-production agreements, and US-aligned security partnerships"; financial commitments described as "geopolitical insurance"; verdict: sovereign ambitions "have yet to fully take off" and the pivot from China to the US deepened reliance on Washington. — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai)
- Hybrid case: India–UAE deal (signed Feb 2026 at the AI Impact Summit, formalised during Modi's May visit to Abu Dhabi): G42 + MBZUAI + Cerebras to build a national-scale AI supercomputer in India with 8 exaflops, Emirati capital and American chips, under Indian governance and data-localisation rules; G42 reportedly building a "global network of AI facilities" it builds, owns and operates for national governments. — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai)

**(b) "How Middle Powers Can Survive the AI Race" — Alasdair Phillips-Robins, Teddy Tawil, Sam Winter-Levy, 28 October 2026 (Technology and International Affairs Program).**
- Two driving variables: "the speed of frontier progress" and "the 'frontier premium,' the gap between the best systems and open-source or other cheap alternatives." — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios)
- Four scenarios (verbatim names) and definitions — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios):
  - "Chokepoint World" (fast progress, large gap): one firm or small group builds hard-to-copy systems; labs bar distillation; US keeps its chip lead via export controls. "The world will divide into AI haves and have-nots." Middle powers with supply-chain leverage, compute or strong security ties have most bargaining power.
  - "Transform or Die" (fast progress, small gap): advanced AI can be copied/built relatively easily by countries with compute and talent; the "bottleneck is not access to AI but the speed at which institutions can adopt it."
  - "Adopt for Advantage" (slow progress, small gap): AI useful but "jagged"; frontier not much better than second-best; sovereign and open-model efforts most viable here. "Moving slowly—Europe's great sin—would be far less costly here than in a rapid-progress world."
  - "Tiered Access" (slow progress, large gap): progress plateaus but a few leaders keep a durable edge; access restricted, less aggressively than in Chokepoint World; building independent frontier models "particularly inadvisable."
- Robustness ranking (no numeric ranking in text; ordering implied by text and Figure 4 caption): (1) chokepoint leverage — "finding sources of leverage at supply chain chokepoints is valuable in almost every technological future"; (2) adoption capacity — pays off in most scenarios but hard to execute through industrial policy; (3) sovereign frontier models — least robust, payoff limited to "Adopt for Advantage". Figure 4 caption: "investments in leverage and adoption pay off in more scenarios than investments in sovereign AI". — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios)
- Six middle-power types (Figure 3 caption, verbatim list): "Semiconductor chokepoint powers, energy-rich compute hosts, physical and industrial powers, talent- and software-rich fast followers, regulated-sector incumbents, and AI-exposed services exporters." — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios)
- Cost marker: building a frontier-equivalent sovereign model "could cost as much as $900 billion", mostly compute and talent. Conclusion: "The best path across all scenarios demands smart engagement, not autarky." — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios)

**(c) "The Compute Coalition: How to Build the Future of AI in the Free World" — same three authors, June 2026.** Not a typology; an economic siting model ("Carnegie Endowment International AI Data Center Model": 100 MW hyperscaler greenfield AI data centre, 12-year life cycle, GB200 NVL72) ranking ten countries (US, UAE, India, Canada, Finland, France, South Korea, Australia, UK, Germany) on modelled competitiveness; "The United States and the United Arab Emirates (UAE) top our country competitiveness rankings, in large part because major projects move fastest in those countries"; Germany "fares worst". — [Carnegie PDF](https://assets.carnegieendowment.org/files/Phillips-Robins%20et%20al._Compute_final.pdf)

**(d) "Operationalizing AI Sovereignty Through Agency, Interoperability, and Openness" — Luca Belli (FGV), 3 September 2026.** Three pillars: Agency ("the substantive capacity to exercise autonomous choice within a state of interdependence"), Interoperability (agency's "primary technical enabler"), Openness (not neutral; a strategic tool). Uses the eight-layer "KASE" framework: data; software and algorithmic models; computational capacity; meaningful connectivity; energy; education and research; cybersecurity; AI-safety frameworks. AI sovereignty = "the capacity of a given country to understand, develop, and regulate digital or AI systems". — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/09/operationalizing-ai-sovereignty-through-agency-interoperability-and-openness)

**(e) Gulf pieces.** Carnegie Middle East (Camille Ammoun, "Exporting Fossil Fuels Through the Cloud", Jan 2026) frames the GCC as hosts of the digital economy's physical backbone rather than creators of AI systems (search summary). — [Carnegie Diwan](https://carnegieendowment.org/middle-east/diwan/2026/01/exporting-fossil-fuels-through-the-cloud). A May 2025 Emissary piece calls Gulf states "geopolitical swing states" for chip deals (search summary). — [Carnegie Emissary](https://carnegieendowment.org/emissary/2025/05/ai-chip-trump-gulf-uae-saudi-security-risk-good-deal)

#### 1.2 Tony Blair Institute — "Sovereignty in the Age of AI: Strategic Choices, Structural Dependencies and the Long Game Ahead" (January 2026)
- Authors: Hilda Barasa, PeiChin Tay, Keegan McBride, Alexander Iosad, Jakob Mökander; foreword Tony Blair; 69 pp. — [TBI PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026); landing page [TBI](https://institute.global/insights/tech-and-digitalisation/sovereignty-in-the-age-of-ai-strategic-choices-structural-dependencies)
- Definition: "Sovereignty in the age of AI is not a binary condition to be achieved or lost. It is fundamentally a question of agency and choice – the ability of a state to make deliberate, future-oriented decisions about how AI is integrated, governed and used in line with its national goals." — [TBI PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026)
- Trilemma: "pursuing control by investing in domestic capability, accessing frontier capability through global systems, and ensuring coherence across regulatory, industrial, fiscal and diplomatic strategies. No state can maximise all three simultaneously." — same PDF
- "Control, Steer, Depend (CSD)" taxonomy (verbatim headings/definitions) — same PDF:
  - "Control: Direct Command Over Strategic Systems" — "ensuring direct ownership, legal authority or exclusive operational command over AI infrastructure, data sets or governance mechanisms." "control is not synonymous with nationalisation or autarky."
  - "Steer: Shaping Outcomes Without Full Ownership" — "Governments steer through regulation, procurement, partnerships and the development of standards that help shape markets and norms."
  - "Depend: Managing Reliance on External Actors" — "reliance on foreign providers for core elements of the AI stack"; split into "maker dependence" ("active and negotiated, with governments co-developing systems or securing technology transfer") and "taker dependence" ("passive, with systems adopted wholesale, with little to no domestic capacity to modify, audit or govern them").
  - "Control, Steer and Depend are not rungs on a ladder, nor a linear progression from 'less sovereign' to 'more sovereign'."
- Unit of analysis: posture per layer of a seven-layer stack. Layer names as used in the Annex tables: Compute infrastructure; Energy; Data; Models; Applications; Talent and skills; Governance. — [TBI Annex PDF](https://images.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/2Atf3gozyxkZd9eTCc69kg/83873c62030f6fe87695f46510b78c33/Sovereignty_in_the_Age_of_AI_-_Annex_-_TBI_-_Jan_2026.pdf)
- ALL SEVEN levers (verbatim, executive summary) — [TBI PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026):
  1. "Secure access to frontier AI models and compute."
  2. "Accelerate AI adoption and diffusion across sectors."
  3. "Aggregate and signal national demand to shape the AI market."
  4. "Treat interoperability as a core component of sovereignty."
  5. "Build and scale smaller, efficient and contextually relevant models."
  6. "Invest in talent and state capacity."
  7. "Align AI infrastructure with sustainable energy planning."
- Four country "pathways" (the closest TBI gets to archetypes; verbatim leads) — same PDF:
  - "advanced economies such as Japan and France assert sovereignty through fallback capacity and governance credibility."
  - "capital-rich countries such as the UAE pursue sovereignty through state-led investment and ownership, building direct control over data, model development and energy, while striking high-value partnerships with frontier actors on favourable terms."
  - "large developing economies such as India and Brazil exercise sovereignty through steering power, using their sizeable digital markets, growing technical talent bases and assertive state direction".
  - "developing digital economies such as Kenya advance sovereignty through negotiated dependence".
- Six case-study labels (verbatim): "selective fallback capability in Japan, frontier acceleration in the UAE, negotiated interdependence in Kenya, regulatory power in France, digital-public-infrastructure-led scaling in India and state-guided co-creation in Brazil." Case headings: "UAE: FRONTIER ADJACENCY THROUGH ACCELERATED STATE INVESTMENT"; "JAPAN: RESILIENCE-FIRST APPROACH THROUGH SELECTIVE INVESTMENT AND PARALLEL CAPABILITY STRENGTHENING"; "KENYA: PROGRESSIVE CAPACITY BUILDING THROUGH SEQUENCED, NEGOTIATED INTERDEPENDENCE"; "INDIA: SOVEREIGNTY THROUGH SCALE, DIGITAL PUBLIC INFRASTRUCTURE AND LAYERED CONTROL"; France and Brazil headings begin "SOVEREIGNTY THROUGH PUBLIC-LED INFRASTRUCTURE AND…" and "SOVEREIGNTY THROUGH REGULATORY STRENGTH AND…" (remainder not captured). — same PDF
- TBI compute index: "State of Compute Access: How to Bridge the New Digital Divide" (Dec 2023; Omdia audit of 55 countries across 25 indicators in seven categories — category names not retrieved) and "State of Compute Access 2024: How to Navigate the New Power Paradox" (18 Nov 2024) (search summary). — [TBI 2023](https://institute.global/insights/tech-and-digitalisation/state-of-compute-access-how-to-bridge-the-new-digital-divide); [TBI 2024](https://institute.global/insights/tech-and-digitalisation/state-of-compute-access-2024-how-to-navigate-the-new-power-paradox). The Jan 2026 paper cites them only in passing: "Only 32 countries worldwide host AI-specific data centres, leaving around 160 nations dependent on foreign infrastructure. TBI's State of Compute Access reports in 2023 and 2024 have found similar trends". — [TBI PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026)

#### 1.3 Oxford Internet Institute — Lehdonvirta, Wú & Hawkins, "Compute North vs. Compute South: The Uneven Possibilities of Compute-based AI Governance Around the Globe" (AIES 2024)
- Authors: Vili Lehdonvirta (OII/Aalto), Bóxī Wú (OII), Zoe Hawkins (ANU). Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society, 2024. — [AAAI](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/31683); full text [Oxford ORA PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:6306c118-58ca-49ba-b7a0-4ee7b9423d5a/files/ssx61dp632)
- Verbatim categories (abstract): "the world is divided into 'Compute North' countries that host AI compute relevant for AI development (ie. training), 'Compute South' countries whose AI compute is more relevant for AI deployment (ie. running inferencing), and 'Compute Desert' countries that host no public cloud AI compute at all." — [ORA PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:6306c118-58ca-49ba-b7a0-4ee7b9423d5a/files/ssx61dp632)
- Operational rule: Compute North = "15 other countries besides the U.S and China that also host at least some quantity of the GPUs most relevant to AI development, namely A100s and H100s. All of these first-tier countries save for India are located in the so-called Global North." Compute South = "A second tier of 13 countries hosts compute of a type more suited for AI system deployment than for development. All of these countries are situated in the Global South, save for Switzerland" (i.e., V100-only). Compute Desert = "all of the remaining countries in the world. These countries host no public cloud AI compute at all, whether for training or for deployment." — same PDF
- Governance meaning: Compute North countries "are positioned to use their territorial jurisdiction to intervene in AI development at the point at which models are sent to their local public cloud regions for training"; Compute South countries can "gatekeep which AI systems can be deployed locally, but less so for shaping AI system development." The Desert "contains a number of rich countries, but it also contains all of the world's lower middle-income and lower-income countries" (IMF classification); rich Desert countries can offset via "wealth sufficient to build government-owned compute capacity". — same PDF
- Follow-up: Hawkins, Lehdonvirta & Wu, "AI Compute Sovereignty: Infrastructure Control Across Territories, Cloud Providers, and Accelerators" (2025): splits compute sovereignty into three levels — territory hosting compute, nationality of the cloud providers owning the data centres, nationality of the accelerator vendors; nine cloud providers; press coverage reports 225 cloud regions, 132 with AI accelerators, in 33 countries (search summary; not verified against the paper). — coverage: [ITPro, 25 Jun 2025](https://www.itpro.com/infrastructure/ai-infrastructure-global-divide)

#### 1.10 Chatham House — "How middle powers can weather US and Chinese AI dominance" (16 February 2026)
- Four pathways (search summary; page blocked): specialize (in a part of the global AI supply chain; trade specialised capabilities or strategic assets such as location, market access or energy for access/investment), align (with the US or China — described as most coherent for many but leaving junior partners little room), share (pool sovereignty with trusted partners; EU the most developed example), hedge (draw on AI capabilities from several nations). #### 1.11 German Marshall Fund — "Middle Powers' Bounded Ambition in AI" (Sharinee Jagtiani and Kristina Kausch, 22 Oct 2026)
- "AI middle power" = a state lacking dominance across the AI value chain but holding strategic assets in one or more segments. Asset categories (Table 1): Material (natural inputs, infrastructure); Industrial and Knowledge (manufacturing, data, research, firms); Institutional and Market (regulation, rule-setting, adoption). Leverage types: diversification, denial, conditioning, bargaining. #### 1.15 European Commission Cloud Sovereignty Framework (DG DIGIT, Version 1.2.1, October 2025) — a public grading scheme
- Eight Sovereignty Objectives with scoring weights (verbatim): SOV-1 Strategic Sovereignty 15%; SOV-2 Legal & Jurisdictional Sovereignty 10%; SOV-3 Data & AI Sovereignty 10%; SOV-4 Operational Sovereignty 15%; SOV-5 Supply Chain Sovereignty 20%; SOV-6 Technology Sovereignty 15%; SOV-7 Security & Compliance Sovereignty 10%; SOV-8 Environmental Sustainability 5%. — [EC Cloud Sovereignty Framework PDF](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/09579818-64a6-4dd5-9577-446ab6219113_en?filename=Cloud-Sovereignty-Framework.pdf)
- SEAL definitions (verbatim) — same PDF:
  - "SEAL-0 No Sovereignty: Service, technology or operations under exclusive control of non-EU third parties, governed entirely in non-EU jurisdictions."
  - "SEAL-1 Jurisdictional Sovereignty: EU law formally applies with limited practical enforceability; service, technology or operations under exclusive control of non-EU third parties."
  - "SEAL-2 Data Sovereignty: EU law applicable and enforceable, with material non-EU dependencies remaining; service, technology or operations under indirect control of non-EU third parties."
  - "SEAL-3 Digital Resilience: EU law applicable and enforceable, EU actors exercising meaningful but not full influence; service, technology or operations under marginal control of non-EU third parties."
  - "SEAL-4 Full Digital Sovereignty: Technology and operations under complete EU control, subject only to EU law, with no critical non-EU dependencies."
- Logic: twofold assessment. SEAL "is used as a Minimum Assurance Level… Tenders that do not offer the required (minimum) levels of assurance consistently across all objectives will be rejected." Separately a weighted "Sovereignty Score" (sum over SOV-1..8 of score/max score × weight) "contributes to the quality score of the tender, as an Award Criterion." Weighting rationale: procedure "already contains significant safeguards" on SOV-2 and SOV-7. Sources drawn on: CIGREF Trusted Cloud Referential v2, Gaia-X, ENISA/NIS2/DORA, France's "Cloud de Confiance", Germany's "Souveräner Cloud". — same PDF
- 48 criteria across the eight categories; SEAL-2 = data sovereignty, SEAL-3 = technological autonomy, SEAL-4 = full sovereignty (Commission explainer, 1 June 2026, which also links "Cloud Sovereignty Framework – Implementation guidance"). — [EC explainer](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/sovereign-cloud-framework-explained-2026-06-01_en)
- First tender result (17 April 2026; up to EUR 180 million over 6 years; four contracts): Post Telecom-led partnership (with OVHcloud and CleverCloud), STACKIT (Schwarz Group) and Scaleway (Iliad) reached SEAL-3; Proximus-led partnership (using S3NS — Thales/Google Cloud JV — plus Clarence and Mistral) reached SEAL-2; minimum for eligibility was SEAL-2. ## 2. What classification logic and data are used?

### Takeaway
Only a minority of frameworks are data-driven. The hard-data ones use public-cloud region censuses (Oxford), composite indices (UNCTAD, World Bank via Tortoise, IMF, Oxford Insights, Stanford), project databases (CNAS, RAND-affiliated data-centre review) or a siting cash-flow model (Carnegie). Most think-tank typologies (TBI, Carnegie scenarios, Chatham House, GMF, Brookings) are qualitative judgements applied per stack layer or per strategic asset.

### Cited Findings
- Oxford "Compute North/South": census of cloud regions of six hyperscalers (AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Alibaba Cloud, Huawei Cloud, Tencent Cloud) "as of October 2023"; 187 cloud regions in 39 countries, 101 GPU-enabled; GPU types recorded as available yes/no per region (H100 2023, A100 2020, V100 2017; H800/A800 for China — none found); quantity of GPUs not observable ("treated as highly confidential"); complemented by 10 expert interviews (Dec 2023–Feb 2024). Unit of analysis: cloud region → country. Global totals: 9 H100-enabled, 48 A100-enabled, 81 V100-enabled regions; "30 have cloud regions that feature" GPUs. — [ORA PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:6306c118-58ca-49ba-b7a0-4ee7b9423d5a/files/ssx61dp632)
- Limitation acknowledged by the authors: study covers public cloud only, not private clusters or government/scientific supercomputers (section "Public Cloud vs. Private and Government Compute Globally"). — same PDF
- TBI CSD: qualitative assignment of Control / Steer / Depend (with Maker/Taker subtype) to each of seven layers per country, with written justification; six case studies (Japan, UAE, Kenya, France, India, Brazil); "Source: TBI analysis". — [TBI Annex](https://images.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/2Atf3gozyxkZd9eTCc69kg/83873c62030f6fe87695f46510b78c33/Sovereignty_in_the_Age_of_AI_-_Annex_-_TBI_-_Jan_2026.pdf)
- Carnegie four scenarios: 2×2 on speed of frontier progress × frontier premium; strategies assessed qualitatively across scenarios; indicators to watch: persistent distillation success (small gap), strong unit economics at top firms (large gap), a formal US access regime (restrictions), diminishing returns on training runs (slowing progress). — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios)
- Carnegie Compute Coalition: discounted life-cycle value model of a 100 MW AI data centre across ten countries; key driver is time-to-power ("speed of deployment matters more than energy costs or tax incentives"); UAE lead time ~22 months vs US ~24 months. — [Carnegie PDF](https://assets.carnegieendowment.org/files/Phillips-Robins%20et%20al._Compute_final.pdf)
- UNCTAD: 2×2 using two proxy indicators per leverage point, cut at global averages; data 2023 or latest. Frontier technologies readiness index: 170 economies, five sub-indices, 0–1 score, four percentile groups. — [UNCTAD TIR 2025](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/tir2025_en.pdf)
- World Bank: readiness level (High/Medium/Low) by "Scale" and "Intensity" computed from Tortoise Media's Global AI Index, 2024. — [World Bank Overview](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/d2ac1ea9-b70e-4080-b5de-8b31098e992f/content)
- Oxford Insights 2025: 195 governments, six pillars, 14 dimensions, 0–100 scores; methodology updated in 2025 so not comparable with earlier editions. — [Oxford Insights](https://oxfordinsights.com/ai-readiness/government-ai-readiness-index-2025/)
- Stanford HAI brief: structured review of public information on flagship "sovereignty"-branded initiatives of Nvidia, Microsoft, Google, AWS, OpenAI (inclusion only when explicitly framed as sovereignty, e.g., Nvidia deployments explicitly called "AI factories"), plus a non-comprehensive review of smaller firms; data as of June 2026. — [HAI brief PDF](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai-issue-brief-the-commercial-landscape-of-ai-sovereignty-offerings.pdf)
- CNAS Sovereign AI Index: 184 projects, data current to June 2026; NVIDIA supplies GPUs for 45% of tracked infrastructure projects (the Carnegie June essay cites CNAS at 52% — an earlier data cut); over three-fifths of projects disclose a foreign partner and four-fifths of those involve a US company; Middle East and East Asia >80% of disclosed investment; UAE and Japan nearly two-thirds; Meta's Llama base for 14 of 25 model projects disclosing a base model. — [CNAS](https://interactives.cnas.org/?p=2715); 52% figure in [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai)
- EU Cloud Sovereignty Framework: questionnaire answers + supporting evidence + public documentation; SEAL per objective as pass/fail floor ("Material weaknesses identified in one or more contributing factor will result in lowering the overall assurance level"); weighted Sovereignty Score as award criterion. Contributing factors include change-of-control assurances, EU financing share, exposure to "US CLOUD Act, Chinese Cybersecurity Law", customer-held cryptographic keys, EU-based operations staff, hardware/firmware origin, open licences, and "Degree of EU independence in high-performance computing capabilities, including processors, accelerators". — [EC Framework PDF](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/09579818-64a6-4dd5-9577-446ab6219113_en?filename=Cloud-Sovereignty-Framework.pdf)
- Ada Lovelace: desk mapping of public compute initiatives across six jurisdictions on five design dimensions. — [Ada PDF](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Policy-briefing-Mapping-global-approaches-to-public-compute.pdf)
- IMF AIPI: 174 countries, four dimensions, simple average, 0–1 (search summary). — [IMF](https://imf.org/external/datamapper/datasets/AIPI)

### Inferences
- The Oxford categories are frequently quoted as if current, but the underlying census is October 2023 and public-cloud only. Gulf sovereign builds since 2024 (G42/Core42, HUMAIN, Stargate UAE) are by construction outside its scope (non-hyperscaler or later).
- Index-based groupings (UNCTAD, World Bank, Oxford Insights) measure general readiness/adoption capacity; none measures ownership or control of infrastructure, which is what consultancy "sovereign AI archetypes" typically classify. Only the EU SEAL scheme, HAI's tiers, the Oxford three-level follow-up and the 775-data-centre study measure control/ownership.

### Gaps
- Exact indicator lists and weights for Oxford Insights 2025, the World Bank readiness cut-offs, and the IMF AIPI were not retrieved.
- The EU tender's per-provider numeric Sovereignty Scores were not published in the sources read; only SEAL levels.

## 4. What does each framework recommend to middle-income and emerging economies, and what does it say about national champions, state ownership, sovereign wealth funds, telecom operators, open-source models, regional pooling?

### Takeaway
The common prescription is selective capability plus negotiated access: prioritise adoption/diffusion, open and small models, interoperability and exit rights, and pool demand or infrastructure regionally. Explicit endorsement of state-owned national champions is rare; the UAE's state-capital model is described as fast but still dependent, SWFs are treated as an enabler specific to capital-rich states, and telecom operators appear mainly as the local "sovereign operator" in vendor (Nvidia, hyperscaler) packages.

### Cited Findings

**Recommendations to emerging/middle-income economies**
- World Bank: "A key strategic decision for governments is whether to focus on building domestic compute capacity (for example, data centers) or securing affordable access to international cloud services. Promoting domestic infrastructure can spur local economic development, job creation, and sovereignty, but it requires affordable energy, robust grids, and sufficient local demand—conditions often lacking in LICs or MICs." Medium-readiness countries should "invest in domestic data centers, as well as form strategic partnerships with foreign technology providers", "customizing open-source models"; "Prioritizing the 4Cs is a no-regret investment strategy." — [World Bank Overview](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/d2ac1ea9-b70e-4080-b5de-8b31098e992f/content)
- World Bank interventions: "cofund regional data centers (for example, African Union initiatives); provide compute subsidies or vouchers for small and medium enterprises (SMEs), start-ups, and researchers (for example, the Republic of Korea's credits); and partner with technology firms to deploy affordable GPUs, potentially through regional collaborations that can create economies of scale." — same PDF
- UNCTAD: catch-up policies "tailored" rather than one-size-fits-all; trajectory laggard → practitioner → leader; three leverage points infrastructure, data, skills; "Countries can leverage private companies to improve their digital infrastructure". — [UNCTAD TIR 2025](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/tir2025_en.pdf)
- TBI: "national competitiveness in the AI era will depend as much on deploying AI widely across the economy and public sector as on building frontier models themselves. For many countries, this path will deliver far greater returns than entering the resource-intensive race to train the world's most advanced systems." Kenya as the emerging-economy template: "negotiated dependence… shaping the terms on which external capacity is deployed, rather than passively absorbing it." — [TBI PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026)
- Carnegie (Oct 2026): act under uncertainty, hedge, preserve optionality; build chokepoint leverage or hold scarce resources; promote adoption capacity (high-skilled immigration, education, flexible labour markets, openness to startups); subsidised public compute for researchers/startups in adoption-led strategies; engage through partnership — example of Hyundai licensing Mitsubishi engine technology rather than building from scratch. — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios)
- Carnegie (Jun 2026): reduce exposure across the stack, invest in sovereign capacity in specific sectors, partner with like-minded countries, preserve flexibility; the India–UAE deal shows a host can keep data/governance control while using foreign capital and expertise. — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai)
- Carnegie/Belli (Sep 2026): "cooperative digital sovereignty" — states "often lack the resources and scale to build AI ecosystems independently"; pool via shared infrastructure, aligned regulation and joint capacity-building through the UN, Digital Cooperation Organization and regional development banks (IDB, NDB, ADB, AfDB); pursue "strategic scale" in application layers, domain-specific models, public-sector AI, hardware niches and open-source ecosystems; three tests for any "sovereign" initiative: does it increase independent choice, reduce lock-in, preserve openness without new vulnerabilities. — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/09/operationalizing-ai-sovereignty-through-agency-interoperability-and-openness)
- Brookings (Feb 2026): map dependencies by layer; prioritise feasible interventions; diversify suppliers and partners; build interoperability and portability via standards, procurement and governance. — [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/is-ai-sovereignty-possible/)

- Stanford HAI: "avoid treating sovereignty as an end goal and prioritize solutions that expand strategic choice"; "building targeted domestic capacity where critical while leveraging external capabilities where efficient." — [HAI brief PDF](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai-issue-brief-the-commercial-landscape-of-ai-sovereignty-offerings.pdf)
- AU Continental AI Strategy: includes a recommendation on an integrated hardware/software environment for AI workloads; AU officials in 2025 urged renewable-powered data centres and regional compute hubs, noting Africa has ~1% of global AI compute (search summary). — [AU PDF](https://au.int/sites/default/files/documents/44004-doc-EN-_Continental_AI_Strategy_July_2024.pdf); [AU-PIDA](https://www.au-pida.org/news/africa-declares-ai-a-strategic-priority-as-high-level-dialogue-calls-for-investment-inclusion-and-innovation-leveraging-artificial-intelligence-ai-for-africas-prosperity-and-collaboration/)

### Inferences
- For a middle-income country such as Morocco, the institutional consensus points to: cloud/partner access plus selective domestic data-centre investment (World Bank "medium readiness"), open-weight/small models (TBI lever 5), demand aggregation and regional/African pooling (TBI, World Bank, AU), and contractual safeguards (portability, audit, non-interruption) rather than a state-owned full-stack champion.
- For Saudi Arabia and the UAE, the institutional reading is that state capital and SWFs buy speed, hosting leverage and "frontier adjacency", not independence; several sources recommend they treat hosting as bargaining leverage (Carnegie "compute hosts", GMF "bargaining") and warn about security and jurisdictional exposure (IISS, Carnegie Compute Coalition).
- Telecom operators are endorsed by vendors, not by think tanks: HAI treats the telco-as-sovereign-operator model as a vendor go-to-market strategy that relocates rather than removes dependence.

### Gaps
- No institutional source found gives an explicit, evidence-based verdict on whether SWF-owned national champions (G42/MGX, HUMAIN/PIF) outperform alternative ownership models.
- Smart Africa, ODI, UNDP and ITU recommendations specific to sovereign AI were not retrieved.
- AU strategy detail on pooled compute rests on search summaries.

## 5. Where do these frameworks agree and disagree with each other and with consultancy framings (e.g. on whether building sovereign infrastructure is worthwhile)?

### Takeaway
There is near-consensus that full-stack sovereignty is infeasible and that the goal is managed/calibrated interdependence; the disagreement is over how much domestic infrastructure is worth building. Carnegie, TBI, Brookings and Stanford HAI are sceptical of sovereign frontier models and of "full-stack" packages, whereas EuroStack, the EU Cloud Sovereignty Framework, the UK roadmap and the Oxford compute-geography work treat territorially and legally controlled infrastructure as valuable in itself; critical scholars go further and call much commercial "sovereign AI" sovereignty washing.

### Cited Findings

**Points of agreement**
- Full-stack sovereignty is out of reach: Brookings "full-stack AI sovereignty is structurally infeasible for almost any country"; TBI "Full self-sufficiency is too expensive, too slow and, for most countries, simply impossible"; Carnegie "attaining complete sovereignty is unrealistic for most nations"; CNAS "it is hard to envision any sovereign compute project outside of China wholly independent of the U.S. technology stack"; GMF full-stack autonomy out of reach. — [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/is-ai-sovereignty-possible/); [TBI PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai); [CNAS](https://interactives.cnas.org/?p=2715); [GMF](https://gmfus.org/news/middle-powers-bounded-ambition-ai)
- Shared vocabulary of "managed interdependence": Carnegie (Joshi), Brookings, GMF; HAI's "calibrating interdependence"; TBI's "deliberate interdependence"/"structured interdependence"; UK: "we do not define sovereignty as isolation or self-sufficiency". — same sources; [UK Compute Roadmap](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68791dc82bad77c3dae4dddb/uk_compute_roadmap_print-version.pdf)
- Sovereignty is a spectrum assessed layer by layer, not a binary (TBI, Brookings, Carnegie, HAI, EU SEAL scale). — [HAI brief PDF](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai-issue-brief-the-commercial-landscape-of-ai-sovereignty-offerings.pdf); [EC Framework PDF](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/09579818-64a6-4dd5-9577-446ab6219113_en?filename=Cloud-Sovereignty-Framework.pdf)
- Adoption/diffusion matters at least as much as building (TBI lever 2; Carnegie robustness ranking; Brookings "a capability to deploy"; World Bank 4Cs "no-regret"). — [TBI PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios); [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-national-ai-plans-get-wrong-and-how-to-fix-them); [World Bank Overview](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/d2ac1ea9-b70e-4080-b5de-8b31098e992f/content)
- Local hosting ≠ control: 48% of non-US data-centre projects (by value) run by US operators; HAI: sovereignty claims focus "less on the origin of the components and more on where the stack is deployed and under whose legal jurisdiction it operates"; EU tender showed Google-technology S3NS reaching only SEAL-2. — [arXiv 2508.00932](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00932); [HAI brief PDF](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai-issue-brief-the-commercial-landscape-of-ai-sovereignty-offerings.pdf); [EC news](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/commission-advances-cloud-sovereignty-through-strategic-procurement-2026-04-17_en)

**Points of disagreement**
- Is sovereign infrastructure/model-building worthwhile?
  - Sceptical: Carnegie — "investments in leverage and adoption pay off in more scenarios than investments in sovereign AI"; frontier-equivalent model up to $900 billion. Brookings — "sovereign AI systems may lead to stranded or underused investment"; fully sovereign stacks risk duplication and incompatible standards. TBI — "resist the urge to own every model and data centre"; "Isolation and protectionism do not protect sovereignty; they weaken it." — [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios); [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/is-ai-sovereignty-possible/); [TBI PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026)
  - Supportive of building: EuroStack — without it "Europe risks becoming a 'digital colony'"; €300 billion programme. Oxford — territorial compute gives regulatory leverage ("States that can exercise territorial jurisdiction over physical compute infrastructures are better placed to impose their rules"). UK — capacity must be "physically located in the UK… not just capacity rented from abroad". World Bank — domestic data centres appropriate from medium readiness upward. — [EuroStack PDF](https://www.bertelsmann-stiftung.de/fileadmin/files/user_upload/EuroStack__2025_final.pdf); [ORA PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:6306c118-58ca-49ba-b7a0-4ee7b9423d5a/files/ssx61dp632); [UK Compute Roadmap](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68791dc82bad77c3dae4dddb/uk_compute_roadmap_print-version.pdf); [World Bank Overview](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/d2ac1ea9-b70e-4080-b5de-8b31098e992f/content)
  - Conditional: Carnegie Compute Coalition sees hosting compute as leverage "roughly in proportion to their share of global compute" and finds the UAE the most competitive non-US site, but warns that "A heavy concentration in the Gulf would bring additional geopolitical risks the market may not price in" and questions whether Gulf grids can supply the scale. — [Carnegie PDF](https://assets.carnegieendowment.org/files/Phillips-Robins%20et%20al._Compute_final.pdf); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios)
- Is dependence acceptable? TBI: "Dependence is not inherently negative"; EU SEAL treats non-EU control as the thing to be graded down, with SEAL-4 requiring "no critical non-EU dependencies"; CISPE regards admitting US-technology offers at SEAL-2 as institutionalised "sovereignty washing" (search summary). — [TBI PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026); [EC Framework PDF](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/09579818-64a6-4dd5-9577-446ab6219113_en?filename=Cloud-Sovereignty-Framework.pdf); [Raconteur](https://www.raconteur.net/data-sovereignty/data-sovereignty-washing-is-an-increasing-risk-europe-cant-afford-to-ignore)
- Open source: HAI and TBI promote it as the main lever at the model layer; Belli warns openness "is not a neutral benefit"; Carnegie makes its value contingent on the frontier premium. — [HAI brief PDF](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai-issue-brief-the-commercial-landscape-of-ai-sovereignty-offerings.pdf); [Carnegie/Belli](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/09/operationalizing-ai-sovereignty-through-agency-interoperability-and-openness); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios)
- Who the sovereign is: Tech Policy Press argues national AI clouds create "a permanent, unelected techno-bureaucracy" and "domestic dependencies"; the "Sovereignty Theater" paper says commercial sovereign AI yields operational but not epistemic sovereignty (search summary). — [Tech Policy Press](https://techpolicy.press/how-national-ai-clouds-undermine-democracy); [AAAI](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/43200)
- UAE verdicts differ: TBI is positive ("turning external reliance into a managed asset rather a vulnerability"); Carnegie (Joshi) says ambitions "have yet to fully take off" and reliance on Washington has deepened; IISS stresses jurisdictional, technological and security dependencies on the US (search summary). — [TBI PDF](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai); [IISS](https://www.iiss.org/globalassets/media-library---content--migration/files/publications/strategic-comments-delta/2026/06/iiss_gulf-ai-infrastructure-and-the-limits-of-technological-sovereignty_08062026.pdf)

**Against consultancy/vendor framings**
- Stanford HAI directly critiques the commercial "full-stack sovereignty" pitch: "These initiatives present a fundamental paradox: They promise that countries will own their AI stack, but at the same time they deepen dependencies on U.S. Big Tech"; "many sovereignty initiatives have been criticized for 'sovereignty washing'"; "Fullstack, cross-layer sovereignty offerings in particular may deepen these dependencies." — [HAI brief PDF](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai-issue-brief-the-commercial-landscape-of-ai-sovereignty-offerings.pdf)
- Brookings: national plans err by replicating "a generic 'AI stack' (compute, chips, foundation models, generic regulation)" rather than building on existing sector strengths. — [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-national-ai-plans-get-wrong-and-how-to-fix-them)
- Consultancy-adjacent convergence: the WEF–Bain paper argues sovereignty should not mean controlling the whole value chain, and BCG (2026) titles its piece "AI sovereignty is an illusion, resilience is real", arguing full-stack autarky is an illusion for most countries (both search summaries). WEF also reports sovereign AI offerings cost 10–30% more than global alternatives and migrations take three to four years (search summary). — [WEF](https://www.weforum.org/publications/rethinking-ai-sovereignty/); [BCG](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/ai-sovereignty-is-an-illusion-resilience-is-real); [WEF story](https://www.weforum.org/stories/digital-trust-and-safety/ai-sovereign-resilience)
- Belli/Carnegie tests for sovereignty washing: "localized cloud infrastructure that depends on foreign providers" and "national" AI systems built on externally controlled models are flagged; any initiative that restricts a nation's ability to choose should be viewed with scepticism regardless of its label. — [Carnegie/Belli](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/09/operationalizing-ai-sovereignty-through-agency-interoperability-and-openness)

### Inferences
- Relative to consultancy frameworks that classify countries by how much of the stack they own or build, institutional frameworks (a) refuse a single ladder (TBI explicitly: postures "are not rungs on a ladder"), (b) rank leverage and adoption above ownership (Carnegie), and (c) grade *control quality* (jurisdiction, operator nationality, supply chain) rather than capacity (EU SEAL, Oxford three-level sovereignty, 775-data-centre study). A consolidated view should therefore present ownership-based archetypes alongside at least one control-quality axis and one adoption axis.
- The benchmark champions outside the US/China/Japan that institutions repeatedly use as reference cases are the UAE (capital-rich accelerator/compute host), France (public-led infrastructure plus regulatory power; Mistral), India (DPI-led scale and steering), Korea (chokepoint plus state-selected model teams), Singapore (governance/fast follower) and Brazil (regulatory steering/co-creation). Saudi Arabia is treated as a follower of the UAE template; Germany as a chokepoint holder with weak siting competitiveness; Turkey and Morocco are largely unclassified.
- Timing matters for reading the sources: frameworks published after mid-2026 (Carnegie Oct 2026, HAI Jul 2026, GMF Oct 2026, CNAS Aug 2026 update, IISS Jun 2026, Carnegie Compute Coalition Jun 2026) incorporate the 2026 Gulf security shock and OpenAI's shelving of Stargate UK, and are noticeably more cautious about Gulf concentration and vendor-led sovereignty than January 2026 papers (TBI, WEF–Bain).
