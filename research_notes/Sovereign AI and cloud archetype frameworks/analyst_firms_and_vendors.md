## 1. What exactly are the archetypes/models (verbatim names) and how is each defined?

### Takeaway
Three families of typology exist, each with a different unit of analysis: countries (IDC's three national AI-sovereignty archetypes; STL's five levels of political driver), deployments/workloads (IDC's ten "sovereign deployment archetypes"; Lünendonk's seven provider models; european.cloud's four US-hyperscaler models; the hyperscalers' own three-tier portfolios) and telecom operators (Twimbit's "four strategic archetypes"; STL's routes). No analyst publishes a typology of "national cloud champions" as such; the closest named categories are IDC's "Domestic public cloud" and "Sovereign partnership" archetypes, Lünendonk's German "Superscaler", and Microsoft's "National Partner Clouds".

### Cited Findings

#### 1A. IDC, "AI sovereignty: national economic competitiveness and security" (blog, 12 Feb 2025) — unit of analysis: country / regional bloc
- Author: Massimiliano Claps, Research Director, Worldwide National Government Platforms and Technologies, IDC Government Insights; published February 12, 2025; no sponsor (IDC blog). — [IDC](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/ai-sovereignty-national-economic-competitiveness-and-security)
- Three comparison dimensions for national AI-sovereignty policy: "strategic posture" (long-term national commitment), "AI governance" (how policymakers decide), "programs" (how the vision is executed). — [IDC](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/ai-sovereignty-national-economic-competitiveness-and-security)
- Archetype 1 "Global AI Powerhouses": only the U.S. and China. They aim for dominance, can act unilaterally and influence partners bilaterally, prioritise frontier over responsible innovation, have scale to invest across the whole AI value chain and can impose "made in …" procurement. — [IDC](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/ai-sovereignty-national-economic-competitiveness-and-security)
- Archetype 2 "Aspirational AI Leaders": "countries or regional blocs like" the U.K., the EU and Japan. Aspire to leadership but lack scale to dominate alone; invest selectively (R&D compute, national security/defence, critical infrastructure, public-sector use cases); keep markets open to compliant foreign suppliers; multilateral responsible-AI stance; favour open source; strict data protection (GDPR, EU AI Act). — [IDC](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/ai-sovereignty-national-economic-competitiveness-and-security)
- Archetype 3 "Regional Dynamos": Saudi Arabia, India, Türkiye and Russia. Aim to be the kernel of regional AI economies under their political influence; mostly opportunistic governance/collaboration (Russia more aligned with one powerhouse); welcome foreign suppliers to fill supply-chain, compute and talent gaps but use regulatory and financial incentives to require local investment, hiring and partnerships. — [IDC](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/ai-sovereignty-national-economic-competitiveness-and-security)
- No further categories are named in the post (three archetypes only). No survey data; the figures cited are contextual (Stargate US$500bn over four years; EU InvestAI €200bn; Saudi 2030 goal). — [IDC](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/ai-sovereignty-national-economic-competitiveness-and-security)

#### 1C. IDC, "Dispelling the myth of a silver bullet in sovereign AI" (blog, 30 Mar 2026)
- Authors: Massimiliano (Max) Claps and Rahiel Nasir (Research Director, European Cloud Practice, Lead Analyst, Digital Sovereignty); March 30, 2026; unsponsored blog. — [IDC](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/dispelling-the-myth-of-a-silver-bullet-in-sovereign-ai/)
- Thesis: "There is no one-size-fits-all approach"; deployment options run on a spectrum from public cloud to fully air-gapped; the model should be matched to each use case or even to individual components of one use case (AI training, RAG, agentic orchestration layer); hybrid architectures are the dominant pattern; leaders are combining global hyperscaler capabilities with local control layers into "sovereign-ready environments"; "sovereignty is not about isolation. It is about choice and control." No formal named archetype list in this post and no provider is named. — [IDC](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/dispelling-the-myth-of-a-silver-bullet-in-sovereign-ai/)
- Data cited: IDC 2025 Digital Sovereignty survey (900+ IT and business leaders): 37% say on-premises is currently their main environment and sovereign cloud is or will be their only cloud type; 55% say sovereign cloud "is, or will be, part of a multicloud or hybrid strategy". Predictions: by 2028 multinational CIOs increase investment in modular, sovereign-ready cloud and data-localisation environments by 65%; by 2026, 55% of governments adopt hybrid sovereign cloud stacks. — [IDC](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/dispelling-the-myth-of-a-silver-bullet-in-sovereign-ai/)

#### 1D. IDC sovereignty taxonomy, "Digital Sovereignty Stack" and AI-sovereignty definition
- IDC definition (2026): digital sovereignty is "the capacity for digital self-determination by nations, organizations, and individuals. It spans data, technical, operational, assurance, supply chain, and geopolitical dimensions." (PDF read) — [IDC/Equinix PDF](https://www.equinix.com/content/dam/eqxcorp/en_us/documents/resources/analyst-reports/ar_idc_missing_layer_sovereignty_en.pdf)
- "IDC's Digital Sovereignty Stack" (source: IDC's Worldwide Sovereign Cloud Taxonomy, 2024, IDC #US50699324), six layers bottom-up with three outcome bands: Self-determination = DATA ("Privacy; data transfers; regulations; portability; security software"), TECHNICAL ("Protection for all digital infrastructure; interoperability; security hardware"), OPERATIONAL ("Controls & stability; authorized key access for customers and admin & support"); Self-sufficiency = ASSURANCE ("Assured workloads; business continuity & disaster recovery; redundancy; portability") and SUPPLY CHAIN ("Chip manufacture; raw materials; energy; skills"); Survivability = GEOPOLITICAL ("Economy; trade dependence; military"). (PDF read) — [IDC InfoBrief sponsored by Google Cloud, Sept 2025, IDC #EUR253788325](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/idc_sovereign_cloud_solutions_infobrief_2025.pdf)
- IDC's three cloud-sovereignty requirement definitions: "Data sovereignty: IT and services that provide a holistic view of how data is collected, classified, processed, stored, managed, and monitored to ensure that regulatory compliance is always met"; "Technical sovereignty: controls applied to all digital infrastructure and services used for cloud-based data and workloads"; "Operational sovereignty: ensuring the transparency of all solutions that control cloud operations, from provisioning and performance management to monitoring physical and digital access to infrastructure". (PDF read) — [IDC/Google Cloud InfoBrief](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/idc_sovereign_cloud_solutions_infobrief_2025.pdf)
- That InfoBrief ("Choosing a Sovereign Cloud Solution", September 2025, authors Rahiel Nasir and Massimiliano Claps, sponsored by Google Cloud) reports 37% of organisations globally currently using sovereign public cloud solutions and 44% planning to; top partner attributes: ownership of in-country data centres (47%), country-level certifications for cybersecurity & cloud (42%), sovereign control over all network infrastructure & connectivity options (39%), solutions to support operational resilience (37%), freedom from lock-in (37%). (PDF read) — [IDC/Google Cloud InfoBrief](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/idc_sovereign_cloud_solutions_infobrief_2025.pdf)
- IDC definition of AI sovereignty: "a nation's or organization's control over the entire AI ecosystem, including training data, models, algorithms and compute, within the laws of the jurisdictions where AI is built and used"; source document "Navigating AI Sovereignty: What's Important for the Global CIO" (IDC #US54419826, March 2026), which describes "IDC's Sovereign AI Framework"; IDC "describes sovereignty as a spectrum of options rather than a binary choice" and "expects most enterprises to seek a hybrid approach between global AI providers and smaller national providers". — [IDC definition page](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/definitions/what-is-ai-sovereignty/)

## 2. What classification logic is used (control over data, operations, technology, jurisdiction, ownership)?

### Takeaway
Nearly every framework uses a layered "control" logic — data → technical → operational → (legal/jurisdictional) — and the most recent ones (IDC Oct 2026, Twimbit Aug 2026, Lünendonk Mar 2026) add legal entity/jurisdiction, network/transit, and exit optionality as separate axes; ownership of the technology stack is the dividing line that keeps hyperscaler-based models out of "full" sovereignty in the european.cloud and CISPE views.

### Cited Findings
- IDC (Oct 2026): eight attributes — legal entity and jurisdiction; datacenter infrastructure; data, technical and operational sovereignty controls; network sovereignty/data in transit; ecosystem access/cloud adjacency; optionality/adaptability — applied per deployment archetype and per workload; "Each archetype is delivered on someone's facility, over someone's network, inside someone's legal entity. Those three factors, rather than the product name, decide what the customer actually controls." Decision sequence: "Classify the workload, fix the jurisdiction, then choose the venue, the legal entity, and the path that delivers it." (PDF read) — [IDC/Equinix PDF](https://www.equinix.com/content/dam/eqxcorp/en_us/documents/resources/analyst-reports/ar_idc_missing_layer_sovereignty_en.pdf)
- IDC's "Eight questions to put to any provider" (one per attribute), e.g. legal entity: "Which entity holds the contract, where is it incorporated, where do its subsidiaries operate, and what happens if its parent is directed to act?"; optionality: "What does exit cost in money, time, and engineering effort… and what has to be rebuilt if our jurisdictional requirement changes in 18 months?" (PDF read) — [IDC/Equinix PDF](https://www.equinix.com/content/dam/eqxcorp/en_us/documents/resources/analyst-reports/ar_idc_missing_layer_sovereignty_en.pdf)
- IDC (taxonomy): six-layer stack Data / Technical / Operational / Assurance / Supply chain / Geopolitical mapped to Self-determination → Self-sufficiency → Survivability. (PDF read) — [IDC/Google Cloud InfoBrief](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/idc_sovereign_cloud_solutions_infobrief_2025.pdf)
- IDC (Feb 2025, countries): strategic posture, AI governance, programs. — [IDC](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/ai-sovereignty-national-economic-competitiveness-and-security)
- european.cloud: who owns the service, who operates it, and who controls the software; test questions are who owns the service, who can press the kill switch, and who gets the money; full technical sovereignty requires control and ownership of the cloud software — "This is not the case for any of the so-called sovereign US models." — [european.cloud](https://european.cloud/sovereign-us-cloud/)
- Directions on Microsoft: classifies by threat rather than by control layer — regulatory risk, unwanted data access, service disruption by a foreign government — plus cost/limitations per option. — [Directions on Microsoft](https://www.directionsonmicrosoft.com/reports/evaluating-digital-sovereignty-options/)
- Lünendonk: four dimensions (operational, technological, data, legal) and provider model relevance rated separately for business-critical/sensitive vs non-critical workloads. (PDF read) — [Lünendonk PDF](https://www.luenendonk.de/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Luenendonk-Studie_Digitale-Souveraenitaet_f.pdf)
- STL Partners: degree of sovereignty ladder (storage → processing → operational → technological) and "sovereignty through control" vs "sovereignty through diversification"; sovereign AI = AI systems + data + infrastructure. (PDF read) — [STL/Red Hat deck](https://stlpartners.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/presentations/2026-06-16+STL+Partners+&+Red+Hat+Telco+Sovereignty+webinar+presentation.pdf); [STL Partners report page](https://stlpartners.com/research/navigating-sovereign-ai-telco-strategies/)
- Twimbit: layers of the AI stack the operator controls ("Data, compute, models, silicon, operations and legal jurisdiction") and own-versus-orchestrate; "Sovereignty is not binary. A country can control its data but not necessarily its silicon." — [Twimbit](https://twimbit.com/blogs/telcos-are-building-sovereign-ai-now-comes-the-hard-part)
- Microsoft: operator of the environment (Microsoft / customer / national partner) is the tiering variable. — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sovereign-clouds/microsoft-sovereign-cloud)
- Forrester (as reported): data residency, operational independence, key control, applicable jurisdiction, third-party access, local support, supply-chain management and ability to operate in a crisis. — [cloudnews.tech](https://cloudnews.tech/forrester-places-sovereign-cloud-at-the-center-of-cloud-strategy/)
- European Commission: 48 criteria in eight categories, SEAL-2 data sovereignty / SEAL-3 technological autonomy / SEAL-4 full sovereignty (search summary). — [European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/sovereign-cloud-framework-explained-2026-06-01_en)

### Inferences
- "Technical/technological sovereignty" (control of the stack from chips to software to models) is the scarcest attribute: IDC rates every market-labelled sovereign archetype L except "Sovereign partnership" (M), including "Domestic public cloud" (L). This implies IDC does not consider a domestic provider's nationality sufficient for technical sovereignty — a domestic cloud built on imported chips/software scores no better than a hyperscaler region on that axis.
- Jurisdiction (legal entity) and operations (who runs the control plane) are the two axes on which a national champion or telecom operator structurally outscores a hyperscaler; ecosystem breadth and optionality are the axes on which it underscores.

### Gaps
- No framework found publishes numeric weights across the control dimensions other than the EU SEAL scheme, whose full criteria were not retrieved.

## 4. What does each framework imply about the viable role of a national cloud champion or telecom operator, and what trade-offs (cost premium, feature lag, lock-in) does it quantify?

### Takeaway
The consensus is a hybrid/"connected" model in which the domestic operator's defensible role is the legal-entity, operations, network and orchestration layer — often on top of hyperscaler or Nvidia technology — rather than a full-stack substitute; buyers will pay roughly a 10–15% premium and accept complexity but will not accept capability loss, and telco-run GPU/sovereign capacity is forecast to remain a single-digit share of the market.

### Inferences
- Demand evidence points to the "Sovereign partnership"/JV archetype as the commercially strongest role for a national champion: it is the top-rated model for critical workloads in DACH (55% very relevant) and tied for second in IDC's global adoption data (35%), whereas pure local providers hold only 5% of most-sensitive data as sole provider (IDC) and European providers hold ~15% share (Synergy).
- The quantified ceiling on premium (about 10–15%) combined with near-zero tolerance for capability loss (93%) implies a domestic champion cannot fund a feature gap through pricing; it must either license hyperscaler technology or restrict itself to workloads where IaaS-level parity suffices.
- Telco analysts (Twimbit, Omdia, Analysys Mason, GSMA Intelligence) agree sovereignty is the entry point but not a sufficient business case; expected operator share of GPUaaS in 2030 is roughly US$8–9.6bn, i.e. around 6–7% of the market.
- For GCC benchmarking: IDC's Middle East/Türkiye/Africa data (48% current use; 51% say AI sovereignty offerings fall short; 39% cite network/data-in-transit concerns — highest of any region) suggests the regional gap is in sovereign AI platforms and sovereign connectivity, both areas where a telecom-rooted champion has assets.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies the feature lag of partner-operated clouds (e.g. months behind, % of services) beyond qualitative statements; Oracle states EU Sovereign Cloud offers "more than 200 of the same services" but no gap versus public OCI.
- A widely repeated "20–40% sovereign premium" figure appeared only on a low-authority blog without attribution (search summary) and is not used.
- Synergy Research has not, in sources found, sized each sovereign model separately; no 2026 Synergy data found.
- Gartner's regional table could not be read on gartner.com (HTTP 403); regional values come from republished copies and search summaries.

### Inferences
- Equinix sponsorship: the framing that sovereignty is decided in the network/interconnection layer and that hosted private/colocation gives the highest control with high optionality (archetype 8 scores H on optionality and is the only non-customer-owned archetype with all-H intrinsic scores) matches Equinix's commercial position; the underlying ten archetypes are IDC's own syndicated work and less exposed to that bias.
- Lünendonk's partners are integrators that profit from multi-cloud complexity and hyperscaler-partner models; the finding that hyperscaler-with-local-operator and German managed-infrastructure providers are most relevant is consistent with their business, though it is survey data.
- Hyperscaler typologies define sovereignty by controls they can deliver (boundary, keys, local staff, partner operation) and omit software ownership — precisely the criterion european.cloud and CISPE use to deny them full sovereignty.
- Telco-focused analysts sell to operators; their consistent caution on economics (Twimbit, Omdia, Analysys Mason) therefore runs against their audience's interest and is more credible for it.
- Sample bias: IDC/Equinix covers 16 countries incl. Middle East/Türkiye/Africa but country bases are 30–50 ("indicative"); IDC/Cohere covers only four Western countries; Lünendonk covers DACH only — none is GCC-specific.

### Gaps
- Sponsor status of the STL Executive Briefing, the GSMA Intelligence AI-factory report and the ABI Research report could not be confirmed beyond the absence of a stated sponsor on their public pages.
- Forrester's vendor-participation and fee disclosures for the Wave were not accessible.
