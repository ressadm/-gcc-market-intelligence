# G42 leads today; HUMAIN must still deliver

*Evidence cut-off: 15 November 2026. Starting point: Lucidity Insights, "G42 vs HUMAIN: Inside the UAE and Saudi Arabia's Race for Sovereign AI" (9 November 2026).*

## 1. Executive summary

**G42 is ahead of HUMAIN on every measure that can be verified in mid-November 2026, but its lead is smaller, more conditional and more exposed than the Lucidity article suggests, and neither company has yet switched on the gigawatt-class AI capacity both have been announcing since May 2025.** G42 has eight years of operating history, two listed subsidiaries with audited numbers (Presight: AED 2.15bn revenue in the first nine months of 2026, actual; Space42: US$404m, actual), about **300 MW of operating data-centre capacity in the UAE** through Khazna (actual, per its chief commercial officer in August 2026 — not the "nearly 650 MW" used elsewhere), a US shareholder and director (Microsoft), and since 10 July 2026 licence-free access to advanced US chips. HUMAIN, 18 months old, has disclosed **1.1 MW of live Nvidia cloud capacity** (actual), an unquantified AMD deployment, 1,024 Qualcomm accelerators, no revenue, no valuation, and case-by-case US licences capped at the November 2025 authorisation of up to 35,000 GB300-equivalents ([Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/abu-dhabi-5gw-uae-us-ai-campus-on-track-regional-conflict); [FWDstart](https://www.fwdstart.me/p/humain-starts-ipo-preparations-16-months-after-launch-with-revenues-still-undisclosed); [Federal Register via govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-14/html/2026-14132.htm)).

The plain conclusions are these. First, the article's "access versus scale" thesis is useful framing but its numbers are not reliable: of roughly 45 claims checked, about two-thirds are confirmed, while HUMAIN's targets are outdated (the current company target is about **3 GW by 2030**, not 1.9 GW), the Aramco deal is misdated (the 49% stake and US$2.6bn cash were disclosed at closing on **1 November 2026**, not agreed in October 2025), and several round figures (US$150m of Huawei equipment, 100,000 chips for 200 MW, "first Arab nation" in Country Group A:5) cannot be traced to a reliable source. Second, G42's regulatory advantage has an expiry date: the G42/Core42 authorisation lapses on **6 April 2027** unless the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) says otherwise, and the rule's preamble says that if they "fail to become U.S. companies" by then they must apply for authorisation. Third, Stargate UAE's first 200 MW, promised for Q3 2026, had **no published commissioning notice** by 14 November 2026, and the 5 GW campus is reportedly being redrawn into dispersed, hardened sites after Iranian threats. Fourth, HUMAIN's evidenced, dated pipeline through end-2027 sums to under 0.5 GW at most, against 3 GW by 2030; its capital to date is entirely Saudi-state-linked; and its clearest customers are partly companies it has invested in. Fifth, Saudi Arabia's long-run fundamentals (land, power system, citizen talent base, domestic market, distance from Iran for western sites) are stronger than the UAE's, but its fiscal position is weaker and every independent forecast found puts Saudi capacity later than the UAE's.

On the scorecard in Section 23, **G42 leads today on eight of fourteen dimensions, HUMAIN on none outright, with the remainder level or unproven**; on the long-run view HUMAIN/Saudi Arabia holds the stronger structural position on four (physical scale, energy, home-grown talent, domestic market) and G42/UAE on five (commercial maturity, US integration, frontier-lab ties, model research, international reach), with five open. No independent source retrieved ranks HUMAIN ahead of G42 on delivered capability; the pro-Saudi case is about 2028–2034. The three dated tests that will decide whether the gap holds are the commissioning of Stargate UAE's first 200 MW, HUMAIN's actual energised megawatts at end-2026 against its 250 MW claim, and what happens on 6 April 2027.

## 2. Scope, labels and how the evidence was handled

This report synthesises four sets of research notes compiled on 15 November 2026 (an extraction and fact-check of the Lucidity article; a G42 deep dive; a HUMAIN deep dive; and a UAE–Saudi context note) together with two earlier reports and two earlier note files in this project. Where the new notes correct the earlier material, the new notes are used and the correction is stated. The Lucidity article is used for framing and chronology only; every figure is taken from the primary or tier-1 sources identified in the notes.

**Status labels on money and capacity figures.** *Actual* = completed, operating or reported in filings or by the party. *Committed* = signed, contracted or closed financing (ceilings are stated as "up to"). *Announced* = target, plan, pledge or programme headline. *MoU* = memorandum, framework, term sheet or "subject to final agreements". *Reported* = press citing unnamed sources. *Estimate* = third-party or derived figure, including this report's own arithmetic.

**Source flags carried from the notes.** **[SS]** = the fact was taken from a search-result summary and the underlying page was not opened; it needs a click-through check before the figure is quoted externally. **[LR]** = low-reliability, single-author or aggregator source. **[1S]** = single-source claim. Unflagged facts come from pages the researchers opened. Several load-bearing pages (AGBI, DCD, Commerce, GlobeNewswire, WAM) blocked automated access, so facts from them are mostly [SS].

**What the report does not do.** It does not fill gaps from memory, it does not cite items the notes could not source (a Hindi model attributed to G42; G42 partnerships with Mastercard, Telecom Italia or AWS), and it sets aside one unverified, out-of-scope headline that surfaced incidentally in one note. Programme headlines that overlap (for example HUMAIN's Nvidia, AMD, xAI, stc and Together AI programmes) are never summed.

## 3. The article argues the two are "not running the same race"

### 3.1 What it is

The piece is a roughly 2,300-word explainer published and last modified on 9 November 2026, written by Erika Masako Welch, co-founder and chief content officer of Lucidity Insights. It contains no data table, no direct quotation from any named person and no outbound source links; its six hyperlinks all point to Lucidity's own G42 database profile, and its only exhibit is a timeline infographic sourced to "Lucidity Insights Research". Bloomberg, The New York Times and OpenAI are named as sources but not linked. The page promotes Lucidity's "State of AI Report 2026", the official publication of the Dubai AI Festival ([Lucidity article](https://lucidityinsights.com/articles/g42-vs-humain-inside-uae-ksa-ai-race); [author page](https://lucidityinsights.com/author/erika-masako-welch); [report page](https://lucidityinsights.com/reports/the-state-of-ai-2026)).

### 3.2 Thesis and structure

The thesis is that both states are building "a single national company that controls every layer of the stack", but that the two "were born seven years apart, built in opposite ways, and are heading to very different destinations". G42 is a "springboard" selling access to US AI for third markets ("the region's AI platform for the world"); HUMAIN is a Saudi "national operating system" or "AI utility" built for scale. The two aphorisms that carry the argument are "G42 is a portfolio that became a national champion. HUMAIN is a national champion now acquiring a portfolio" and "Saudi Arabia wins by being the biggest market in the system; the UAE wins by being its most connected node." The article runs through eleven sections: key takeaways; introduction; two philosophies; how each was formed; US chips and China ties; G42's companies; where HUMAIN stands; a timeline infographic ("seven years against eighteen months"); "Is G42 ahead of HUMAIN?"; a verdict; and implications for Gulf founders ([Lucidity article](https://lucidityinsights.com/articles/g42-vs-humain-inside-uae-ksa-ai-race)).

Its three key takeaways are that G42 is the first mover with "about ten operating companies, including two listed ones that together earned about $1.4 billion in 2025"; that the UAE "sells access", having "reportedly tor[n] out $150 million of Huawei equipment to win privileged US chip access"; and that Saudi Arabia "bets on scale", with HUMAIN "targeting 1.9 GW of compute by 2030 and 6.6 GW by 2034".

### 3.5 Verdict and predictions

The article says G42 is "further along… on the measures that can be verified today" and that "the gap is less about megawatts than about maturity", gives HUMAIN the lead only on "speed and scale of ambition", and then declares the "who is ahead" question "the wrong one": "G42 has the head start, the revenue and the access; HUMAIN has the scale, the capital and the speed. Each can succeed without the other failing." It makes three forward-looking statements, none dated or falsifiable by year: (1) "If it delivers even half of its 6.6 GW pipeline, it will outbuild the UAE on raw capacity in the next decade"; (2) "The real test of the next six months is… whether each can deliver on its own promise, and which of the two models other nations decide to copy"; and (3) for founders, HUMAIN "will compete with many horizontal AI startups rather than buy from them" — "In Abu Dhabi, plug into access. In Riyadh, build for scale" ([Lucidity article](https://lucidityinsights.com/articles/g42-vs-humain-inside-uae-ksa-ai-race)). The six-month window (to about May 2027) happens to span the 6 April 2027 expiry of G42's chip authorisation, which the article does not identify as the pivotal event.

### 4.3 What the article omits

**The expiry and conditions on G42's chip access.** The article mentions "beyond April 2027" once without explanation and calls A:5 a "standing… rule". The rule text shows a three-tier hierarchy inside the UAE in which G42 and Core42 rank below UAE government agencies and below eight US-headquartered firms, receive approval only for advanced computing items, and lose it on 6 April 2027 absent BIS notice ([govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-14/html/2026-14132.htm)). US political criticism of the rule is also absent ([EnterpriseAM, 13 Jul 2026](https://enterpriseam.com/uae/2026/07/13/us-gives-uae-a-fast-lane-for-ai-chips-and-defense-tech/)).

**The 2026 war.** Iranian strikes on Gulf states began on 28 February 2026; drones hit AWS facilities in the UAE and Bahrain; on 15 September 2026 AWS said it could not restore data hosted exclusively in one UAE availability zone; an Iranian video named Stargate UAE as a target. The article reduces this to "recent geopolitical tensions… including Iranian threats", and does not mention the Houthi strikes on Riyadh and Dammam airports in October 2026 ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/09/17/aws-middle-east-outage-permanent-data-loss-bahrain-uae/); [EnterpriseAM](https://enterpriseam.com/ai/2026/10/15/uae-saudi-arabia-and-qatar-three-strategies-for-the-ai-race/)).

**Evidence against the "different games" split.** Khazna has acquired a Dammam site for up to 200 MW; HUMAIN plans offices in the US, France and the UK and sells HUMAIN ONE globally through AWS Marketplace; HUMAIN has built a platform on a Chinese open-weight model, contradicting "both have paid for that access by turning away from China" ([AGBI](https://www.agbi.com/ai/2025/12/uaes-khazna-enters-saudi-arabia-with-first-data-centre/) [SS]; [Semafor](https://www.semafor.com/article/09/04/2026/the-switzerland-of-ai-saudi-arabias-big-ai-bet)).

**Framing.** The notes infer, without evidence of bias in any single claim, that the "each can succeed without the other failing" conclusion is commercially convenient for a publisher that sells data services in both markets. Lucidity's own database should not be used for G42's valuation (it lists "US$1.5B as of April 2024", which equals Microsoft's investment and is almost certainly a mislabel) or headcount, and its "US$5B+" implied HUMAIN valuation is simple arithmetic on US$2.6bn for 49% that ignores non-cash contributions on both sides ([Lucidity G42 profile](https://lucidityinsights.com/startups/g42-1645); [Lucidity, 2 Nov 2026](https://lucidityinsights.com/articles/aramco-buys-49-of-humain-26-billion)).

Both systems are top-down and personalised, but organised differently. Abu Dhabi concentrates ownership, capital and policy coordination in one sub-sovereign principal, which gives speed and a single counterpart for Washington and creates the conflict-of-interest exposure now targeted by US Democrats. That reading is this report's synthesis of the notes ([EnterpriseAM, 24 Jul 2026](https://enterpriseam.com/menaplus/2026/07/24/how-us-midterm-politics-could-threaten-gulf-ai-ambitions/); [vision2030.ai tracker](https://vision2030.ai/analysis/humain-data-centers-riyadh-dammam-status/) [LR]).

### 8.3 How the models differ, and where they converge

The article's contrast holds on four structural points identified in the notes: owner (ruling-family vehicle with US minorities versus sovereign fund plus national oil company); US technology relationship (embedded and monitored versus licensed customer); operating model (federation of branded companies versus one integrated company); and geography (exporting versus domestic build). It breaks down above the slogan. Both sell build-to-suit capacity to the same pool of US hyperscalers and labs; both depend on the same US licence regime; Khazna is building in Saudi Arabia; and HUMAIN declares export ambitions of its own. Low-authority aggregators offer a further analogy — the UAE as a Singapore-style hub, Saudi Arabia as a Korea-style chaebol model [LR][SS] — which is colour, not evidence ([AiTimeline](https://aitimeline.in/middle-east-ai-race-timeline-saudi-arabia-uae-9819/)).

Published frameworks do not separate the two. The earlier frameworks report found that McKinsey places both countries in "AI hubs – producers for export use", BCG lists both as "Rising contenders" and "GenAI middle powers", and no framework distinguishes HUMAIN from G42. The Tony Blair Institute rates the UAE's compute posture only "Steer" because "reliance on foreign accelerators and hyperscale platforms keeps ultimate control externally anchored", and Carnegie judges that UAE sovereignty efforts "have yet to fully take off" while suggesting sector-specific models "may be more financially feasible" for Saudi Arabia ([TBI](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026); [Carnegie, June 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai); [McKinsey deck](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Ruben-Schaubroeck-Sovereign-AI.pdf)). McKinsey's "producers for export" label describes an intention for Saudi Arabia, not a delivered position.

### 9.2 What the capital picture means

Known G42 debt of about US$3.2bn is small against a US$30bn-plus build. Who funds Stargate UAE — G42 equity, MGX, customer prepayments or project finance — is the largest unknown in G42's finances and the likely driver of the September 2026 capital-raise talks (inference in the notes). On the Saudi side the Infra GPU facility implies about SAR 44m (US$11.7m) per MW of IT load for chips, in line with Amin's unit cost (this report's arithmetic), and the largest external raise reported had no reported close; HUMAIN's financing after the October attacks came from a state fund, not the international lenders implied by the Goldman mandate, though whether that reflects timing or investor caution is unknown. HUMAIN's single largest disclosed cash deployment — US$3bn into xAI — exceeds Aramco's entire 2026 cash contribution. On the public record HUMAIN currently looks more like an investor-developer than an operating cloud business.

Analysts read both companies' moves toward outside capital as a way to "multiply deployed capital without proportionally increasing their sovereign exposure", while noting that "the starting gun on Gulf AI listings might have been loaded, rather than fully fired" and that public investors will want evidence of sustainable revenue (Vijay Valecha, Century Financial) ([AGBI mirror](https://www.century.ae/ar/century-in-news/ai-giants-humain-and-g42-look-to-raise-outside-capital/)).

## 10. Infrastructure: about 300 MW operating for Khazna, 1.1 MW disclosed for HUMAIN, and no gigawatt-class AI site confirmed live on either side

### 10.1 Three conflicts in the capacity numbers, and which figure is better supported

**Khazna's operating capacity.** A December 2025 report gave Khazna "nearly 650 MW" across about 30 live sites ([AGBI](https://www.agbi.com/ai/2025/12/uaes-khazna-enters-saudi-arabia-with-first-data-centre/) [SS]); the earlier project report used that figure. On 13 August 2026 Khazna's chief commercial officer, Greg Jasmin, said the company had 30 live data centres with **about 300 MW operational in the UAE**, almost 370 MW under construction and commissioning, and "almost 700 MW" expected live and contracted within 12–14 months ([Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/abu-dhabi-5gw-uae-us-ai-campus-on-track-regional-conflict)). The two are reconcilable only if the 650 MW counted contracted or under-construction capacity (300 + 370 ≈ 670). **The 300 MW figure is better supported**: it is more recent, comes from a named executive in a page the researchers opened, and separates operating from pipeline. This report uses about 300 MW operating and treats 650 MW as operating plus under construction.

**The UAE's total installed capacity.** One report cites 358 MW for the whole UAE in 2025 ([AGBI](https://www.agbi.com/ai/2026/10/ex-google-chief-warns-gulf-ai-bubble-is-ready-to-burst/) [SS]), which cannot coexist with a 650 MW Khazna but is consistent with a 300 MW Khazna plus other operators. The Gulf Data Center Association put the GCC at about 1 GW installed or under construction at end-2024, about 40% each in the UAE and Saudi Arabia, and Knight Frank says the UAE holds "just over half of live commissioned capacity" in the region [SS] ([Baker Institute](https://www.bakerinstitute.org/sites/default/files/2026-04/20260410_Global%20AI%20Race.pdf); [W.Media](https://w.media/middle-easts-data-center-landscape-poised-for-dramatic-growth-knight-frank/)). Definitions differ and should not be mixed. **The best-supported reading is UAE live IT load of roughly 300–400 MW, with about 370 MW more in build at Khazna, excluding Stargate.** Saudi Arabia's official figure is 467 MW in Q1 2026, up from 68 MW in 2021 [LR for the relay], against an Alvarez & Marsal estimate of about 410 MW IT load across about 40 facilities [SS] ([vision2030.ai tracker](https://vision2030.ai/analysis/humain-data-centers-riyadh-dammam-status/); [Alvarez & Marsal](https://www.alvarezandmarsal.com/node/97616)). On these numbers the two countries' live bases are the same order of magnitude; the UAE's lead is in near-term AI-grade capacity under construction, not a large installed-base gap.

**HUMAIN's 2030 target.** The article and S&P (October 2026) use **1.9 GW by 2030**, a figure from Amin's May and August 2025 statements, alongside 6.0–6.6 GW by 2034 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/08/27/saudi-arabia-wants-to-be-worlds-third-largest-ai-provider-humain.html); [Khaleej Times/S&P](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/business/uae-saudi-arabia-to-add-up-to-3gw-of-data-centre-power-by-2030)). The shareholders' own closing release of 1 November 2026 says **3 GW by 2030**, The National reports about 3 GW by 2030 and more than 6 GW by 2034, and PIF's strategy sets a 3,000 MW national target of which PIF commits 1,800 MW ([Aramco](https://www.aramco.com/en/news-media/news/2026/pif-and-aramco-close-humain-transaction-to-accelerate-humains-global-growth); [The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/future/technology/2026/10/22/saudi-arabias-humain-expands-nvidia-deal-with-84mw-ai-computing-plan/); [TradeArabia](https://www.tradearabia.com/News/414099/PIF-launches-2026-2030-strategy-focus-on-six-integrated-ecosystems)). **About 3 GW is the better-supported current company target** (most recent, primary). The 1.9 GW figure is outdated as a company target but remains in use by S&P as a forecast input; PIF's 1,800 MW commitment is the most authoritative Saudi planning number and is close to it. A single secondary source also reports a build path of 250 MW by end-2026 and 1.7 GW in 2027 [1S], far above evidenced delivery ([EnterpriseAM](https://enterpriseam.com/ai/2026/10/15/uae-saudi-arabia-and-qatar-three-strategies-for-the-ai-race/)).

### 11.2 The July 2026 UAE rule, read from the text

The rule "Enhanced Favorable Treatment for the United Arab Emirates Under the EAR" took effect on 10 July 2026 and was published on 14 July 2026 (91 FR 43034–43039). It removes the UAE from Country Groups D:3 and D:4 and adds it to A:5, with a footnote limiting Licence Exception STA to approved UAE entities. A new Supplement No. 8 to Part 740 creates three tiers: (a) UAE government agencies including the Ministry of Defence, approved for advanced computing items and full STA (state-owned corporations excluded); (b) "Group 42 Holding Ltd (d/b/a G42, including G42 Cloud Technology LLC)" and "Core42 Technology Projects LLC (d/b/a Core42)", approved **only** for advanced computing items; and (c) eight US-headquartered firms — Amazon, Apple, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI Group PBC, Oracle and X.AI — approved for advanced computing items and full STA ([govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-14/html/2026-14132.htm)).

"Absent subsequent notice by BIS", the G42/Core42 authorisation **expires on 6 April 2027**. If they "fail to become U.S. companies on or before April 6, 2027, they will need to apply for authorization" through the advisory-opinion route, decided by the Commerce Secretary with State and the National Security Adviser within 30 days. End-use and end-user controls, export filings and record-keeping remain, and BIS keeps an Export Control Officer in the UAE. The rule does not define "becoming a US company". Commerce tied the change to the UAE's status as a major defence partner ([govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-14/html/2026-14132.htm); [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/g42-us-ownership-chip-access-export-control-endgame)). BIS will also "favorably review" MGX applications ([EnterpriseAM](https://enterpriseam.com/infrastructure/2026/10/28/a-chip-deadline-and-a-war-will-test-how-much-of-its-ai-buildout-the-gulf-controls/)).

**Corrections to the earlier project notes.** The earlier notes could not read the Federal Register and relied on secondary summaries; the new notes read the rule directly. The Core42 entity named is "Core42 Technology Projects LLC", with "G42 Cloud Technology LLC" included under the G42 entry. And statements that the A:5 move "allows up to 500,000 chips a year" conflate the reported May 2025 quota with the rule, which contains no quantity.

What G42 must do to keep access, on the notes' reading: maintain the security and reporting conditions and the US-specified monitoring agreed in February 2026 (which Under Secretary of State Jacob Helberg called "unprecedented"); before 6 April 2027 either restructure into a "U.S. company" or obtain fresh approval; and by implication keep the UAE's reciprocal US investment flowing, since the October 2025 permits were sequenced after it. Just Security adds that officials reportedly promised G42 will become mostly US-owned, that the UAE made no new security commitments, and that the rule removes limits on Chinese remote access that earlier licences imposed; it calls the rule "a remarkable victory" for the UAE and "unlikely to be sustainable" as a preference over Saudi Arabia and Israel ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/g42-us-ownership-chip-access-export-control-endgame); [Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/150631/the-trump-administration-throws-gulf-ai-a-lifeline/)).

### 11.3 Saudi Arabia's different treatment

Saudi Arabia is not in A:5, has no Supplement No. 8 entry, and had no published Saudi rule as of 31 July 2026; shipments are governed by case-by-case licences. No BIS, Commerce or White House statement located addresses why [LR][SS] ([vision2030.ai](https://vision2030.ai/geopolitics/us-chip-export-controls-saudi-uae-divergence/)). Offered explanations are opinion: a six-month timing lag (the UAE framework was agreed in May 2025 and took 14 months to reach the Federal Register); the UAE's Major Defense Partner status against Saudi Arabia's Major Non-NATO Ally designation (18 November 2025); and the BIS officer stationed in the UAE. June Park of Singapore Management University draws the distinction sharply: the UAE holds a standing status under the regulations, Saudi Arabia a licence revocable for a specific product or user that "may be ephemeral", while China ties "don't clearly separate the two" ([EnterpriseAM](https://enterpriseam.com/infrastructure/2026/10/28/a-chip-deadline-and-a-war-will-test-how-much-of-its-ai-buildout-the-gulf-controls/)). The notes record a conflict on the date of the UAE's Major Defense Partner status (September 2024 in one EnterpriseAM piece, July 2026 in another), which is unresolved.

Saudi Arabia's bilateral frame is the Strategic AI Partnership signed on 19 November 2025 by Prince Faisal bin Farhan and Secretary Rubio, alongside a pledge of US$1tn of US investment (announced) and a Section 123 civil nuclear agreement signed on 22 July 2026 [LR for the last] ([AGSI](https://agsi.org/analysis/firmer-details-emerge-on-u-s-gulf-ai-cooperation/); [Nextgov](https://www.nextgov.com/artificial-intelligence/2025/11/us-and-saudi-arabia-commit-new-ai-agreement/409626/)). HUMAIN's licence conditions, per its chief executive, bar training Chinese frontier models on its compute but permit hosting Chinese open-source models in Saudi data centres ([Semafor, 4 Sept 2026](https://www.semafor.com/article/09/04/2026/chinese-tech-firms-deepen-ties-in-saudi-arabia)).

## 22. Competition, cooperation and where Qatar fits

**Where they compete.** The contest is sharpest in four places: US chip allocation and regulatory status; inference-export tenants (Luma, Together AI, Cohere and Nebius in Saudi Arabia against OpenAI and Microsoft in the UAE); migrant AI talent; and third-party capital (MGX's US$49bn fund against HUMAIN's planned US$2.5bn infrastructure vehicle and US$10bn venture fund). HUMAIN will tie venture cheques to use of compute in Saudi Arabia, a direct pull on start-ups that might otherwise locate in the UAE, and the two "data embassy" concepts target the same foreign-government customer ([Semafor, 4 Sept 2026](https://www.semafor.com/article/09/04/2026/chinese-tech-firms-deepen-ties-in-saudi-arabia)). Guney Yildiz of Anthesis warns against treating "Gulf AI capacity" as one pool: renting from an MGX-backed operator and from a HUMAIN campus still being built carry different risks ([EnterpriseAM](https://enterpriseam.com/ai/2026/10/15/uae-saudi-arabia-and-qatar-three-strategies-for-the-ai-race/)).

**Where they cooperate or co-invest.** Cooperation is commercial and mostly one-directional: Khazna has a Dammam site for up to 200 MW and plans about 400 MW of Saudi capacity within its 2030 programme (announced) [SS]; no HUMAIN presence in the UAE was found. Abu Dhabi and Saudi vehicles sit on the same cap tables — MGX and HUMAIN in xAI, now SpaceX; ADIA in MiniMax and a Saudi fund in Z.ai — and third parties such as Pure DC plan in both countries. No government-level AI compact was found. CSIS's Yasir Atalan argues the Gulf should treat AI investment as a shared regional project with joint classification of campuses and trusted backup capacity; that remains an analyst recommendation ([MEED](https://meed.com/uae-saudi-firms-plan-data-centre-projects-in-saudi-arabia); [Baker Institute](https://www.bakerinstitute.org/sites/default/files/2026-04/20260410_Global%20AI%20Race.pdf); [EnterpriseAM](https://enterpriseam.com/infrastructure/2026/10/28/a-chip-deadline-and-a-war-will-test-how-much-of-its-ai-buildout-the-gulf-controls/)).

**Who hosts whom.** The two countries have so far attracted different anchors and have not split one: OpenAI in the UAE; xAI in Saudi Arabia (announced); Microsoft in both (shareholder in G42; a Saudi region due); AWS in both, damaged in the UAE and expanding in Saudi Arabia; Google more visibly in Saudi Arabia; Oracle in both. Anthropic has Gulf shareholders but no confirmed in-country infrastructure in either state ([EnterpriseAM](https://enterpriseam.com/infrastructure/2026/10/28/a-chip-deadline-and-a-war-will-test-how-much-of-its-ai-buildout-the-gulf-controls/)).

**Qatar.** Qatar is a capital-rich third pole that invests abroad and has no operating champion at home. Qai, owned by the Qatar Investment Authority and launched in December 2025, has a US$20bn joint venture with Brookfield (announced) whose first investment, reported on 6 November 2026, is a proposed 1 GW data centre at Drax power station in northern England — US$3.4bn over five years, still needing approvals and an anchor tenant [SS]. Qai's chairman says it will not build large language models. Domestic sovereign GPU capacity is in the tens of megawatts (Syntys: 26 MW operating, 20 MW under construction). QIA has joined three Anthropic rounds, and Qatar needs US export licences for advanced chips. The war hit Qatar hard — about a fifth of LNG capacity damaged — and public spending has been cut while QIA keeps investing ([Semafor](https://www.semafor.com/article/11/06/2026/qai-and-brookfield-back-supersized-uk-data-center); [DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/qatar-launches-ai-infrastructure-investment-venture-following-ai-company-launch/); [EnterpriseAM](https://enterpriseam.com/ai/2026/10/15/uae-saudi-arabia-and-qatar-three-strategies-for-the-ai-race/)). EnterpriseAM's three-way summary is the cleanest: the UAE is in "full alignment with the US"; Saudi Arabia is "building capacity at home, with some distance from the US"; Qatar is cautious, with overseas bets. Kuwait's sovereign fund has also backed a US$10bn AI infrastructure venture with KKR, Nvidia and Vistra (June 2026) ([EnterpriseAM](https://enterpriseam.com/ai/2026/10/15/uae-saudi-arabia-and-qatar-three-strategies-for-the-ai-race/)).

## 25. Strategic implications

**For G42.** The priority is to turn construction into disclosed operation before the April 2027 deadline forces a negotiation from weakness. A published commissioning notice for Stargate UAE's first phase would be worth more to its credibility than any new announcement, and the evidence shows the market has noticed the missing one. Second, the ownership question cannot be deferred: the rule's text makes "become U.S. companies" the default path, and each month without a decision raises the odds that the outcome is set in Washington. A structure that separates the US-chip infrastructure business from the sovereign Emirati businesses is one logical response, but the notes contain no evidence that G42 is pursuing it; that is this report's inference. Third, G42's best protection against both a US policy shift and an AI correction is the part of the group that already earns audited, multi-year revenue. Presight and AIQ show an export model that works with governments; proving the same for Core42 with private customers, and publishing the numbers, would close the gap the article papers over with "US$1.4bn". Fourth, dispersal and hardening should be presented as a design feature with a cost and a schedule, since silence invites the assumption of delay. Finally, G42's foreign programme needs pruning to what is financed: 80 MW in the US and one EU-cleared joint venture are real; Kenya, Vietnam, India and the Italian supercomputer are not yet.

**For HUMAIN.** The binding constraint is credibility, not ambition. The most valuable single act available is disclosure: energised megawatts by site, chips delivered and contracted offtake by counterparty. Madar's audit shows the gaps are "disclosure-driven", which means they can be closed by choice. Second, the financing model depends on arm's-length offtake; Cohere-type contracts with no Saudi investor on the other side are worth more to lenders than larger commitments from investees, and closing the Goldman-advised raise would be the proof. Third, the chip-access gap with the UAE is the strategic variable most worth management attention: a published Saudi rule would convert a revocable licence into a standing status, and the open-weight "Switzerland" stance is the feature most likely to be weighed against it. HUMAIN can keep neutrality at the model layer or seek UAE-style treatment at the chip layer; the evidence suggests the two pull against each other, though no source establishes that as the cause of the difference in treatment. Fourth, the Riyadh concentration of the pipeline is now a priced risk after October 2026; the Red Sea sites that independent analysts rate safest are also the latest in the schedule. Fifth, the domestic structure is unfinished: a year-old unsigned venture with stc, overlapping sovereign-cloud offers to the same government buyers, and an Aramco contribution that has never been itemised. The earlier frameworks report's conclusion still applies: the defensible Saudi role lies in sovereign AI platforms, connectivity and applications more than in one more cloud region.

**For third parties.** Customers, lenders and partner governments should treat "Gulf AI capacity" as two different risk profiles, as Yildiz argues: a US-integrated operator with an installed base, a regulatory cliff date and a named-target flagship; and a state-capitalised developer with larger land and power options, later delivery and no disclosure. Founders are better served by the article's practical advice than by its numbers — partner programmes and sovereign-cloud distribution exist in Abu Dhabi today, while in Riyadh the champion is simultaneously gatekeeper, funder and competitor.

## 28. Conclusion

The article asks the right question and declines to answer it. Read against the evidence, the answer is that G42 is ahead and that its lead is a wasting asset unless it is converted. Every G42 advantage that can be verified is either historical (operating companies, listed revenue, an installed base) or conditional (a licence with an expiry date, a flagship without a commissioning notice, a tenant not yet paying). Every HUMAIN advantage is structural and unrealised (land, power, a larger home market, a locally educated workforce, a sovereign owner that has made it a priority while cutting elsewhere). The race is therefore less "access versus scale" than **conversion versus credibility**: G42 has to convert a regulatory window and a construction site into operating, financed, disclosed capacity before April 2027; HUMAIN has to make megawatts, chips and contracts visible before lenders and Washington decide what its announcements are worth.

Two implications go beyond the comparison. First, the most informative metric in Gulf AI is one neither company publishes: energised megawatts by site. Until that exists, rankings — including the scorecard here — rest on proxies, and the party that discloses first gains a credibility premium at almost no cost. Second, the 2026 war and the July 2026 rule have between them moved the decisive variables out of the Gulf. Whether G42 remains an Emirati company in form, whether Saudi Arabia gets a rule of its own, and whether hyperscalers keep building within drone range are decisions that will be taken in Washington and in the boardrooms of a handful of US firms. Both champions were built to reduce dependence; at the end of 2026 both are more dependent on a single external counterparty than when they started, and the one that manages that dependence better, not the one that announces more, will be ahead in 2030.
