# Frameworks classify countries; champions remain unclassified

*Consolidated industry view of published archetype frameworks for national cloud and AI champions, sovereign cloud and sovereign AI — evidence as of 15 November 2026. Companion to the benchmarking report "National champions rent their sovereignty from Washington"; company profiles are not repeated here.*

## 1. Executive summary

**Roughly eighty publications from consultancies, analyst firms, vendors, think tanks and public bodies were reviewed, and none of them contains an archetype scheme for national cloud or AI champions as companies.** What the literature offers instead is four different kinds of classification that are routinely confused with one another: country strategy archetypes (WEF/Bain's five, McKinsey's A/B1/B2/C, IDC's three, the Tony Blair Institute's four pathways, Carnegie's six middle-power types), provider and telecom-operator typologies (Accenture's four provider types including "Frontliners", EY's four actors, Twimbit's four telecom archetypes, STL Partners' three telecom routes), deployment and control-level archetypes for a single cloud or workload (IDC's ten "sovereign deployment archetypes", Stanford HAI's three tiers, the hyperscalers' own three-tier portfolios, Lünendonk's seven provider models), and grading scales (the EU's SEAL-0 to SEAL-4, SecNumCloud, C5/C3A, and the readiness indices of BCG, UNCTAD, the World Bank, the IMF and Oxford Insights). The company-level archetypes this study defined earlier (C1–C6 and A1–A5) are therefore a genuine addition to the literature, not a restatement of it, and they have to be tested against these four families rather than matched to one of them.

The frameworks agree on substance to a striking degree. Every strategy house, every Big Four firm and every think tank states that full-stack sovereignty is out of reach for all but the United States and China, and prescribes some version of partnership: "strategic interdependence" (WEF/Bain), "resilience" (BCG Henderson Institute), "pragmatic AI sovereignty" (Strategy&), "selective sovereignty" (IBM, Deloitte), "connected sovereignty" (IDC) and "managed interdependence" (Carnegie, Brookings, GMF). They agree that control should be kept over data, identity, critical workloads and sector applications; that cloud platforms and frontier models should be partnered; and that chips cannot be owned. They disagree on how much domestic infrastructure is worth building, on whether hyperscaler technology operated by a local partner deserves the word "sovereign" (the EU graded one such offer SEAL-2 and the European cloud trade body called that "sovereignty washing"), and on the Gulf, where January 2026 papers are admiring and post-June 2026 papers are cautious.

Three findings matter most for defining champion archetypes. First, the coordinator's cloud list mixes two axes that the published frameworks keep apart: who the champion is (state-capital group, telecom, private provider, state IT company) and what it deploys (own stack, licensed hyperscaler stack, hosted hyperscaler region, GPU service). The most corroborated archetype, C5 "Licensed trusted cloud venture", is a deployment model with at least seven published counterparts; the most used, C2 "Telecom-anchored gateway", is an owner type that the telecom literature splits into at least three distinct plays. Second, the AI list is corroborated descriptively but not by name: only Stanford HAI comes close to a typology of state-backed model companies, and no framework recognises Korea's tournament mechanism or distinguishes HUMAIN from G42. Third, no framework measures delivery. McKinsey places Saudi Arabia among "AI hubs – producers for export use" while the earlier benchmarking found 1.1 MW of disclosed live capacity at HUMAIN; BCG's matrix measures readiness, not strategy; and Oxford Insights ranks Saudi Arabia 15th in the world on government AI readiness with a compute-capacity score of 25.94 out of 100.

For Saudi Arabia, the frameworks imply the capital-and-energy hub route (WEF Pathways 4 and 5, McKinsey B2, IDC "Regional Dynamos") with posture set layer by layer as Strategy& describes, but they are silent on the things the benchmarking identified as decisive: verified capacity, the HUMAIN–stc overlap, export-control contingency and disclosure. For Morocco, the frameworks are close to silent altogether: it is named only as a BCG "Gradual practitioner", as part of McKinsey's "Africa" consumer group and in readiness indices. The pathway they imply (WEF Pathway 3, World Bank medium-readiness prescriptions, TBI's "negotiated dependence") matches the earlier report's regulation-first, consortium-based recommendation, and one published provider type, Accenture's "Federated consortia", together with the telecom-led consortia that won two of the four contracts in the EU's first sovereign-cloud tender, gives that recommendation a published precedent. Important flags apply throughout: McKinsey's March 2026 article was not read from the primary, several placements are derived or read from slide images, and two sets of figures in the notes conflict (Section 11).

## 2. Scope, source handling and how to read this report

This report covers every framework recorded in the four research notes for this question, grouped as the notes are: strategy consultancies and the World Economic Forum (Section 3); the Big Four, Accenture and other consultancies (Section 4); analyst firms, telecom analysts and vendors (Section 5); and think tanks, academia and public bodies (Section 6). Section 7 compares them in tables. Section 8 consolidates them by unit of analysis. Section 9 crosswalks them to this study's company-level archetypes. Section 10 draws lessons, including for Saudi Arabia and Morocco. Section 11 lists limitations. Champions in the United States, China and Japan are outside the scope of the study; those countries appear only where a framework uses them as an anchor.

Archetype names are quoted verbatim from the notes. Four access flags are carried through from the notes and matter for how much weight a statement can bear. **(primary)** means the researchers read the original document. **(search summary)** means the page was blocked and the fact comes from a search-engine snippet or a repost; wording is unverified. **(secondary)** means a third party's reading of the framework. **(derived)** means the researchers or this report applied a source's own rule to its data; the source did not state the result. Statements labelled "this report's synthesis" or "this report's application" are not attributable to any publisher.

## 3. Strategy consultancies and the WEF publish four taxonomies that do not map one-to-one

### 3.1 WEF with Bain: five country archetypes and five pathways that all end in the same place

The most developed country scheme is the World Economic Forum white paper "Rethinking AI Sovereignty: Pathways to Competitiveness through Strategic Investments", dated January 2026 and produced by the Forum's AI Global Alliance "In collaboration with Bain & Company" (primary) ([WEF/Bain, January 2026](https://www3.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Rethinking_AI_Sovereignty_Pathways_to_Competitiveness_through_Strategic_Investments_2026.pdf)). The release day of 20 January comes from a search summary. The unit of analysis is the "economy", national or supranational. It defines AI sovereignty as "the ability of economies to shape, deploy and govern AI ecosystems in accordance with their own values, whilst ensuring strategic and operational control, flexibility and, ultimately, resilience through a combination of localized investment and trusted international collaboration."

The five archetypes and their definitions are these. **"Global AI value chain leaders"** are "End-to-end investors across the AI value chain, with world-class capabilities in every element and enabler, pursuing dominance by maintaining or expanding their leadership positions in AI technology and innovation". **"Ecosystem builders"** are "Orchestrators pursuing balanced, government-driven investment strategies, building strong local AI ecosystems and regional influence, and increasingly joining international alliances and partnerships"; their typical profile is "Smaller advanced economies leveraging agile governance and strong access to capital and resources", with "Dense, efficient AI infrastructure serving as regional hubs, with national champions playing key roles (e.g. by partnering with hyperscalers and trusted vendors)". **"Selective players"** are "Targeted players with moderate adoption and selected strengths in the AI ecosystem, investing in sector-specific data and applications", typically "Established industrial economies leveraging strong research and manufacturing bases". **"Adoption accelerators"** are "Rapid commercial deployers with wide AI adoption that focus on driving application development, scaling use cases and demand first, then selectively backfilling AI infrastructure through partnerships", typically "Large emerging economies driven by a fast-growing digital sector and foreign investment". **"Emerging collaborators"** are "Early-stage actors with nascent, fragmented AI capabilities still in the stage of exploring the AI landscape and potential partnerships".

The classification logic is the only explicit rule among the strategy houses. Economies are placed by how many of six value-chain elements they cover (foundational inputs, hardware inputs, infrastructure, data, foundation models, applications and services), "using presence and scale thresholds", against the maturity of enablers such as talent, capital access and connectivity. Coverage of all six makes a leader; "Four to five" an ecosystem builder; "Two to three" a selective player; "Zero to one" an adoption accelerator if enablers are mature and an emerging collaborator if not. The thresholds sit in an endnote the researchers did not extract, so the rule cannot be reproduced.

Placements are shown only as flag icons, which the researchers read visually from the rendered page: the United States and China as leaders; the **United Arab Emirates and Singapore** as ecosystem builders; **Germany and France** as selective players; **India** as an adoption accelerator; Rwanda as an emerging collaborator (high confidence, not text-verified). The text confirms Singapore ("Singapore exemplifies an ecosystem builder") and India. **Saudi Arabia is not assigned an archetype**; it appears through HUMAIN, named among "national cloud providers such as HUMAIN (Saudi Arabia), G42 (United Arab Emirates), Outscale (France) and STACKIT (Germany)" and as "backed by the Saudi Public Investment Fund (PIF)". South Korea is unassigned, described as having "initially concentrated their investments in hardware elements like chips". Qatar, Brazil, Turkey and Morocco do not appear in the notes' record of the paper, and Morocco is confirmed absent by text search.

The five pathways are titled "Pathway 1: From selective players to ecosystem builders"; "Pathway 2: From adoption accelerators to ecosystem builders"; "Pathway 3: From emerging collaborators to ecosystem builders via adoption accelerators"; "Pathway 4: From emerging collaborators to ecosystem builders via selective players"; and "Pathway 5: From emerging collaborators to ecosystem builders". None targets leader status. Pathway 3 tells economies without abundant capital or energy that "Rather than pursuing full sovereign AI infrastructure, these economies can use PPPs, pay-as-you-go pricing models and concessional finance to expand access". Pathway 4, for economies "with high availability of capital, land and energy, strategic location and regional connectivity advantages", recommends that they "invest in excess AI infrastructure capacity and serve both domestic and neighbouring markets as regional compute hubs", secure "trusted technology sourcing, such as advanced chips, and long-term offtake arrangements" and "host external foundation models through partnerships". The paper's headline lines are "Full AI sovereignty is not feasible for economies" and "Partnership, not ownership, is a key path forward".

The paper's interest and limits should be weighed together. Its acknowledged contributors include executives from Mubadala Investment Company, G42, Amazon Web Services, CoreWeave, Palantir, Cloudflare, Cisco and IBM, and Bain advises governments and sovereign funds on these programmes; the conclusion that countries should partner with trusted global providers aligns with those interests (the notes mark this as an inference from the acknowledgement list, not a published critique). The paper itself says archetypes "are not rankings or sequential steps to be followed", yet every pathway ends at the Singapore–UAE model, which gives large industrial economies only one route. Eight flags for five archetypes is a thin base.

The May 2026 follow-up, "AI Infrastructure in the Age of Sovereignty: Requirements, Strategies and a Trusted Framework for Digital Embassies", again with Bain, adds no country archetypes (primary, partially read) ([WEF/Bain, May 2026](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_AI_Infrastructure_in_the_Age_of_Sovereignty_Requirements_Strategies_and_a_Trusted_Framework_for_Digital_Embassies_2026.pdf)). It defines an "AI sovereignty spectrum" between two "reference strategies", **"trusted international partnerships"** and **"extensive domestic ownership"**, and states that "Only a small number of economies – primarily China and the US – can come close to achieving extensive domestic ownership". It is relevant to Saudi Arabia because it describes "Saudi Arabia's draft Global AI Hub Law", which "outlines three types of legally defined hubs": "Private hubs", "Extended hubs" and "Virtual hubs". It also warns that "Growing reliance on increasingly complex financing structures and greater use of debt mean that disruptions or underperformance can have spillover effects across the wider financial system."

### 3.2 McKinsey: two lenses, one read from a conference deck and one not read from the primary

McKinsey's clearest scheme comes from a McKinsey-branded deck, "European Sovereign AI – aspiration or reality?", presented at the Cambridge Risk Symposium on 18 June 2026 and posted publicly by Cambridge Judge Business School despite a "CONFIDENTIAL AND PROPRIETARY" footer (primary) ([McKinsey deck, June 2026](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Ruben-Schaubroeck-Sovereign-AI.pdf)). Its "Sovereign AI nation archetypes" are: **A — "AI Consumers & Challengers in AI"** "(e.g. Africa, Central Asia, Latin America, smaller Southeast Asian economies)"; **B1 — "Targeted Sovereign Players – producers for home use"** "(e.g. EU esp. France, UK, Italy, Japan, India)"; **B2 — "AI hubs – producers for export use"** "(e.g. KSA, Qatar, UAE, Singapore)"; and **C — "Fully Sovereign Builders – producers and exporters of AI"** "(e.g. US, China)". The logic is whether a nation consumes, produces for itself, produces for export or does everything; the supporting statistic is that "Only 30 countries have in-country training or inference capability". No scoring model is disclosed and the placements are "e.g." lists. Germany and South Korea are not named (Germany sits implicitly in "EU"); Morocco, Brazil and Turkey are covered only by region.

The same deck carries three further schemes at other units of analysis. For a cloud or AI offering it gives **"Tier 1: Fully sovereign"**, **"Tier 2: Partially sovereign"** and **"Tier 3: Public stack"**. For organisations it lists "possible plays": governments as "Stack builder" or "Ecosystem orchestrator"; providers ("Makers", "e.g., NeoClouds, TelCos, Software companies, SIs") choosing where to play; enterprise "Takers" as "Full-stack sovereign builder" or "Sovereign AI services consumer"; and investors ("PE/VC, SWF") as **"Strategic champion investor"**. For national strategy it offers a menu of design options, including governance on a scale from "Ministry led command" through "State owned AI operator" to "Public private task force", and supply options "National GPU super clusters", "Local cloud providers", "Chip alliances & regional fabs" and "Sovereign hyperscaler clouds". The deck sizes the "Global sovereign AI market" at roughly US$150–200 billion in 2025 rising to US$500–600 billion in 2030, on a "McKinsey Proprietary Sovereign AI Model"; its own footnote says this is demand, which "differs from actual spend captured by sovereign AI solutions". A firm sizing the market for its own advisory work is an interest to weigh.

McKinsey's March 2026 article, "Sovereign AI: Building ecosystems for strategic resilience and impact", **was not read from the primary**: mckinsey.com returned errors, and everything the notes record comes from a repost or from blogs ([McKinsey article page](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/sovereign-ai-building-ecosystems-for-strategic-resilience-and-impact); [UDLAP repost](https://observatorioglobal.udlap.mx/sovereign-ai-building-ecosystems-for-strategic-resilience-and-impact/)). The only reasonably secure sentence is that jurisdictions follow archetypes "ranging from end-to-end AI hubs and state-led models to industry- and policy-driven regional platforms" (search summary). A blog renders five labels — "Frontier Powerhouses", "State-Led Execution", "Policy-Enabled Hubs", "Industry-Led Platforms", "Research-First Models" — but these are the blog's wording, not confirmed as McKinsey's (secondary) ([Kategos](https://www.kategos.ai/articles/the-2026-ai-industrial-complex-ecosystems-alliances-and-the-shift-to-sovereign-as-a-service)). The publication day is unresolved (3 or 24 March 2026). The repost attributes to McKinsey five partnership models ("Standardized sovereign zones, demand aggregation frameworks, joint operating models, data and model consortiums, and blended finance"), three waves (baseline with lighthouse workloads; shared infrastructure and data; exportable specialisation) and a main failure mode of mis-sequencing, meaning heavy investment before demand, governance and talent are ready (search summary). This report uses the article only with that caveat and does not place countries with it.

### 3.3 BCG: a readiness matrix that is not a sovereignty framework, and an in-house rebuttal of stack sovereignty

BCG's "The AI Maturity Matrix: Which Economies Are Ready for AI?" (November 2024, 73 economies) is the only strategy-house scheme with a transparent, weighted method (primary, read from a mirror because bcg.com blocked access) ([BCG 2024 PDF](https://web-assets.bcg.com/fe/61/6962e74b44328f148c8a9ac1002d/ai-maturity-matrix-nov-2024.pdf); [mirror copy](https://img.cbinews.com/attachment/2025/02/17/uP4faaedZDq5dl6yiKUdXfQ4KaAWOQuXxXDoPmq7.pdf)). It plots exposure to AI against readiness, the latter an "ASPIRE" index of 33 indicators weighted Ambition 10%, Skills 25%, Policy and regulation 10%, Investment 15%, Research and innovation 15% and Ecosystem 25%. Its six archetypes are **"AI pioneers"** ("Economies able to meet high levels of exposure with extremely high readiness"), **"Steady contenders"** ("relatively high exposure to AI and sufficient levels of readiness"), **"Rising contenders"** ("relatively low exposure to AI despite high readiness"), **"Exposed practitioners"** ("relatively high exposure to AI and insufficient levels of readiness"), **"Gradual practitioners"** ("relatively low exposure to AI and low readiness") and **"AI emergents"** ("extremely low readiness and different levels of exposure").

The 2024 placements are complete: Singapore is an AI pioneer; France, Germany and South Korea are steady contenders; **Saudi Arabia, the UAE, India, Brazil and Türkiye are rising contenders**; **Morocco and Qatar are gradual practitioners**. Recommendations follow three themes — "Enabling AI" for emergents, "Accelerating AI" for contenders and practitioners, "Amplifying AI" for pioneers — and only pioneers are told to "Provide tailored support for national AI champions, unicorns, and startups"; contenders and practitioners are told to "Boost investment in high-performance computing and data centers, and attract FDI in AI". The October 2026 edition keeps the structure, moves Japan and South Korea into the pioneer tier, and adds a sovereignty frame: "Sovereignty has four components (chips, compute and cloud, models, and applications), and owning all four is exceedingly rare" ([BCG 2026 press release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/execution-not-ambition-now-separates-ai-economies-as-japan-and-south-korea-join-the-pioneers-302913365.html); [BCG 2026 page](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/countries-most-ready-for-ai)). Its vignettes say "Saudi Arabia pairs a national AI champion with global chip and cloud partnerships" and "France backs a national champion with dedicated compute". The 2026 placements of Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Morocco and others sit in an image exhibit and are **not confirmed**. A BCG "GCC AI Pulse" (2025) reportedly classes Saudi Arabia and the UAE as AI Contenders and Qatar among AI Practitioners (search summary) ([BCG GCC AI Pulse](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2025/the-gcc-ai-pulse-mapping-the-regions-readiness-for-an-ai-driven-future)).

The BCG Henderson Institute supplies two further pieces. "How CEOs Can Navigate the New Geopolitics of GenAI" (9 December 2024) splits the world into **"GenAI superpowers"** (the US and China) and **"GenAI middle powers"** (the EU; Saudi Arabia and the UAE; South Korea and Japan), assessed on capital, talent, intellectual property, data, energy and computing power, and names national champions: Mistral, Aleph Alpha, G42 and TII's Falcon, and Aramco (primary, partial) ([BCG Henderson Institute, 2024](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2024/how-ceos-navigate-new-geopolitics-of-genai)). "For Most Countries, AI Sovereignty Is an Illusion. Resilience Is Real." (25 March 2026) abandons archetypes for "The Four Components of AI Resilience" — "infrastructure, trust and values, adoption pull, and partnerships" — and argues that "The objective is not to catch up with hyperscalers, but rather to ensure that sensitive workloads can run domestically" (primary) ([BCG Henderson Institute, 2026](https://web-assets.bcg.com/pdf-src/prod-live/ai-sovereignty-is-an-illusion-resilience-is-real.pdf)). It warns of "massive sunk costs into stack-based moonshots" and says early sovereign-cloud schemes such as GAIA-X "became mired in complex governance committees and branding exercises". Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Morocco are not used as cases. The limitation of the matrix is self-evident and is the single most common misuse in this literature: it ranks readiness and exposure and is cited as if it ranked sovereignty or strategy.

### 3.4 Bain under its own name publishes no archetypes

Bain's Technology Report 2025 chapter "Sovereign Tech, Fragmented World" (23 September 2025) contrasts regional postures without naming archetypes and addresses companies rather than states; the notes record its wording as a fetch-tool paraphrase ([Bain, 2025](https://www.bain.com/insights/sovereign-tech-fragmented-world-technology-report-2025/)). Bain's archetype thinking is published through the WEF.

## 4. The Big Four, Accenture and other consultancies classify providers, postures and control levels, not countries

None of these firms publishes a country taxonomy comparable to WEF, McKinsey or BCG. Their contribution is at the provider and posture level, which is where champion archetypes live.

### 4.1 Accenture: the only published provider class that means "national champion"

Accenture's global report "Sovereign AI: Own your AI future — From managing risk to accelerating growth" (copyright 2025; survey of 1,928 organisations in 28 countries, July–August 2025) classifies providers into four types (primary) ([Accenture global report](https://www.accenture.com/content/dam/accenture/final/accenture-com/document-4/Sovereign-AI-Report.pdf)). **"Global cloud providers"** "offer unmatched scale, service breadth and pace of innovation." **"Frontliners are country-endorsed national champions (often local incumbents such as telcos or high-tech companies) with local brand recognition that can meet public sector trust requirements and run government-endorsed industry or cross-industry solutions"**. **"Neoclouds are AI-native providers built for the new era of compute"**, with Nebius, Nscale, CoreWeave and Lambda as examples. **"Federated consortia are alliances of governments, industries and technology providers that co-develop shared sovereign infrastructure solutions and governance frameworks"**, with examples including Catena-X, "AI Gigafactory-style consortia emerging across Europe, and national programs such as Saudi Arabia's HUMAIN initiative and Indonesia's Sovereign AI TechCo." Its recommendation is that "The most resilient strategies combine all four as needed", and its headline is that "Only one-third of an organization's AI workloads need to be sovereign."

Its placements are of providers and of a survey index. Frontliner cases are Indosat Ooredoo Hutchison via Lintasarta, Telus, Telia Cygate and Cassava Technologies; STACKIT is described as an enterprise-turned-provider; "Microsoft's Bleu and Delos partnerships" are global-cloud partnerships. A "Sovereignty Maturity Index" averages organisations' self-assessments by country: Nordics 62, Germany 61, France 55, **Saudi Arabia 54**, India 54. Two limits follow from the method. The sample is restricted to organisations "that consider sovereignty highly or critically important to their technology stack", so percentages overstate general concern, and the country scores are averages of self-assessments, not measures of national capability. Accenture is also the delivery partner to at least five of the providers it showcases, so "combine all four" matches an integrator's business model.

Accenture's "Architecting Sovereign AI in the Middle East" (copyright 2026; sample of 185, of which UAE 85, Saudi Arabia 85 and Qatar 15) assesses the region layer by layer and finds progress "strong in governance and data, advancing at the model layer, and still structurally constrained at compute" (primary) ([Accenture Middle East report](https://www.accenture.com/content/dam/accenture/final/accenture-com/document-fy26/q4/Architecting-Sovereign-AI-In-The-Middle-East-Accenture-Report.pdf)). It uses three provider types rather than four and drops the word "Frontliners": "Global hyperscalers", **"National champions and state-backed platforms"**, and "AI-native infrastructure providers ('neoclouds')". It names G42 Cloud, "SCCC Alibaba Cloud, a joint venture between Saudi Telecom Company and Alibaba Cloud", "STC Cloud, and Mobily", and HUMAIN; models ALLaM, Falcon, Jais and Fanar; and applications "Presight AI and Mozn". Its most useful statistic for this study is that **62% of regional executives say local sovereign AI solutions are "behind" or "not competitive"** against hyperscalers, while 60% call their country's approach "highly sovereign". It recommends "concentrating sovereign investment at the application layer". Two cautions: a Qatar sub-sample of 15 supports no country conclusion, and HUMAIN is a "Federated consortia" example in the global report but a national champion in the regional one, so Accenture's own category boundary is unstable.

### 4.2 Strategy&: four postures per layer, written for Gulf governments

Strategy& Middle East's "Pragmatic AI sovereignty: Architecting AI value chains for control and continuity in the Gulf countries" (2026; web release metadata 31 August 2026) is the most directly relevant consultancy framework for Saudi Arabia (primary) ([Strategy& PDF](https://www.strategyand.pwc.com/m1/en/industries/technology/pragmatic-ai-sovereignty/strategyand-pragmatic-ai-sovereignty.pdf)). The unit is a government's posture on each of five value-chain layers (semiconductors and hardware; physical infrastructure; cloud platforms; models and intelligence; AI systems and sector deployment). The four postures are **"Assure"** ("secures a value layer via agreements, allocation, diversification or contractual safeguards"; example: UAE GPU allocations), **"Control"** ("secures a value layer by owning, operating or governing it through government entities or national champions"; example: India's Aadhaar), **"Partner"** ("secures a value layer via partnership with a global player, while maintaining local governance, localization, resilience and exit rights"; example: France's Bleu) and **"Influence"** ("using capital, regulation, procurement or market access to shape the layer's standards, capital flows, supplier priorities or policy dialogues"; example: the EU AI Act). Layers are assessed on "concentration, capital intensity, strategic sensitivity and value creation potential". The colour ratings of the layer-by-posture matrix could not be recovered from the text.

Its recommendations assign roles more explicitly than any other source: "Governments will need to set the rules and aggregate demand. Sovereign wealth funds will need to secure access and wield influence. National champions will need to build and operate critical infrastructure. Government entities and national champions will have to create data and use cases, too." On cloud it says "Refrain from competing with hyperscalers across the full platform stack"; on infrastructure, "Own and operate in-country data centers, power, cooling, interconnection, hardened sites and distributed edge infrastructure through national players"; on models, take "a measured approach to developing frontier models, while investing assertively in sovereign hosting, Arabic adaptation and sector-specific models". It warns that "Taken too far, localized systems can become fragile", citing damage to cloud infrastructure in the UAE and Bahrain. The GCC is treated as one group; no Gulf state is placed individually. The report discloses no sponsor, but an author's biography describes advising "national ICT champions and government entities", and the advice is consistent with that client base. PwC's global alliance pages on cloud sovereignty with Microsoft and AWS were blocked and are known only from search summaries ([PwC–Microsoft](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/services/alliances/microsoft/cloud-sovereignty-without-compromise.html); [PwC–AWS](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/services/alliances/amazon-web-services/digital-sovereign-cloud-strategy.html)).

### 4.3 Deloitte, KPMG and EY: informal country groupings and actor lists

Deloitte Asia Pacific's "Sovereign AI: Realising strategic opportunities across Asia Pacific" (2026) offers eight policy pillars ("Strategy; Governance; Compute; Energy and water; Datasets and models; Workforce; AI ecosystem; Global collaboration") and four descriptive country groups that it does not call archetypes (primary) ([Deloitte APAC](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-zone1/apac/en/docs/collections/2026/sovereign-ai-realising-strategic-opportunities-across-asia-pacific.pdf)). "Large, security-driven economies like China and India are advancing more comprehensive full-stack approaches"; "More globally integrated economies including Singapore, Australia, and New Zealand adopt selective sovereignty"; "Advanced industrial economies such as Japan, South Korea and Taiwan emphasise sovereignty in strategic sectors"; and "Emerging economies including Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, and the Philippines prioritise foundational enablers". Its conclusion is that "Most economies are converging on hybrid models." Deloitte's TMT Predictions 2026 chapter contains no typology but predicts "over US$100 billion will be committed to building sovereign AI compute" in 2026 and notes Cassava's AI factory in South Africa "with plans for Egypt, Kenya, Morocco, and Nigeria", the only mention of Morocco in this group ([Deloitte TMT Predictions](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/technology/technology-media-and-telecom-predictions/2026/tech-sovereignty.html)). Deloitte Luxembourg's "4+1 dimensions" framework ([Deloitte Luxembourg](https://www.deloitte.com/lu/en/our-thinking/future-of-advice/achieving-digital-sovereignty.html)), Deloitte Netherlands' "sovereignty scale" ([Deloitte Netherlands](https://www.deloitte.com/nl/en/issues/generative-ai/soevereine-ai-grip-op-data-modellen-digitale-afhankelijkheden.html)) and a Deloitte-authored Fortune brand-studio piece ([Fortune/Deloitte](https://fortune.com/brandstudio/deloitte-ai/how-sovereign-ai-is-impacting-the-global-enterprise-landscape/)) are enterprise-level. Deloitte Middle East's "Sovereignty in AI and Cloud" for the World Governments Summit 2025 was seen only as an abstract with no taxonomy ([World Governments Summit](https://worldgovernmentssummit.org/observer/reports/detail/sovereignty-in-ai-and-cloud)). Deloitte sells a "Deloitte Sovereign Cloud" offering with ServiceNow and STACKIT ([Deloitte–ServiceNow–STACKIT](https://www.deloitte.com/content/dam/assets-shared/docs/alliances/servicenow/2025/sovereign-cloud.pdf)).

KPMG in India's "Sovereign AI and national security: A background paper" (February 2026) contrasts four national paths by instrument of control: "The U.S. relies on defence partnerships, China relies on state ownership, Europe relies on regulation. India is choosing an infrastructure-led approach." (primary) ([KPMG India](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/in/pdf/2026/02/sovereign-ai-and-national-security.pdf)). It says its own analytical framework "is not detailed in this paper". ## 5. Analyst firms and vendors classify deployments and telecom operators, and their best work is sponsored

### 5.1 IDC: three country archetypes and ten deployment archetypes

IDC's country scheme comes from an unsponsored blog, "AI sovereignty: national economic competitiveness and security" (12 February 2025), written as advice to technology suppliers ([IDC, February 2025](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/ai-sovereignty-national-economic-competitiveness-and-security)). It compares countries on "strategic posture", "AI governance" and "programs" and names three archetypes. **"Global AI Powerhouses"** are only the US and China. **"Aspirational AI Leaders"** are "countries or regional blocs like" the UK, the EU and Japan, which aspire to leadership, invest selectively and keep markets open to compliant foreign suppliers. **"Regional Dynamos"** are **Saudi Arabia, India, Türkiye and Russia**, which aim to be the kernel of regional AI economies and welcome foreign suppliers while using regulatory and financial incentives to require local investment, hiring and partnerships. IDC advises vendors dealing with Regional Dynamos to "selectively co-invest with local partners in compute infrastructure, open innovation hubs, and local talent". There is no survey behind the scheme, and the UAE, Qatar, Singapore, South Korea, Brazil and Morocco are not placed.

IDC's deployment scheme is the most granular in the literature. The InfoBrief "The Missing Layer of Digital Sovereignty — Ten Deployment Archetypes, Eight Attributes, and the Sovereign Controls that Most Organizations Have Not Yet Specified" (October 2026) is **"Sponsored by Equinix"**; the ten archetypes and first five attributes come from an IDC syndicated guide of March 2026 that the researchers did not access directly (primary for the InfoBrief) ([IDC/Equinix InfoBrief](https://www.equinix.com/content/dam/eqxcorp/en_us/documents/resources/analyst-reports/ar_idc_missing_layer_sovereignty_en.pdf)). The unit is a deployment model chosen per workload. The ten, "ordered by degree of customer control from lower to higher", are: 1 "Global public cloud"; 2 "Global public cloud with sovereign controls"; 3 "Global public cloud with dedicated local zones"; 4 "Public sovereign cloud"; 5 **"Sovereign partnership"**; 6 **"Domestic public cloud"**; 7 **"Government cloud"**; 8 "Hosted private environment, including colocation"; 9 "On-premises private environment"; 10 "Air-gapped environment". Archetypes 2–7 are "Typically labeled sovereign by the market". Eight attributes grade each one: "Legal entity and jurisdiction", "Datacenter infrastructure", "Data sovereignty controls", "Technical sovereignty controls", "Operational sovereignty controls", and three added in the sponsored brief, "Network sovereignty and control of data in transit", "Ecosystem access and cloud adjacency" and "Optionality and adaptability over time".

IDC's trade-off matrix, described as an "assessment of the typical posture of each archetype, not of any individual provider", carries the finding that matters most for champion design. "Domestic public cloud" — the national-champion archetype — scores high on legal entity, datacentre, data and operational control but **low on technical sovereignty and low on ecosystem access**. "Sovereign partnership" is the only market-labelled sovereign archetype rated moderate rather than low on technical sovereignty, and IDC notes that in that model "contracts may include provisions allowing the local operator to continue using technical components as open source if the international provider exits". IDC's summary concept is "Connected sovereignty": "The ambition is decision-making autonomy rather than infrastructure autarky".

The survey behind the InfoBrief (June 2026; 633 organisations in 16 countries) supplies demand evidence. Only 5% of the organisations with increased interest will stop using global public cloud vendors and partner only with local providers; 34% will combine local and global providers. Only 7% will accept fewer capabilities than the leading non-sovereign solution. In the Middle East, Türkiye and Africa region, 48% use sovereign solutions, 51% say AI sovereignty offerings fall short and 39% name data in transit, the last two the highest of any region. **The notes flag an unresolved conflict in the premium data**: the source states both that "Fifty-two percent will accept higher subscription costs of up to 10%" and that "66% are prepared to pay a premium above 10% today", with 6–10% the most common single answer; both are quoted as published. The Equinix sponsorship matters: the three added attributes, and the statement that "Colocation and private infrastructure providers, not hyperscalers, sell the highest degree of assured control directly", match a colocation operator's commercial position. Country bases of 30–50 are indicative only, and none is GCC-specific.

Other IDC material is supporting. An unsponsored blog, "Dispelling the myth of a silver bullet in sovereign AI" (30 March 2026), argues that "There is no one-size-fits-all approach" ([IDC, March 2026](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/dispelling-the-myth-of-a-silver-bullet-in-sovereign-ai/)). A Google Cloud-sponsored InfoBrief (September 2025) reproduces "IDC's Digital Sovereignty Stack" of six layers (data, technical, operational, assurance, supply chain, geopolitical) grouped as self-determination, self-sufficiency and survivability ([IDC/Google Cloud InfoBrief](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/idc_sovereign_cloud_solutions_infobrief_2025.pdf)). A Cohere-commissioned InfoBrief (August 2026) is an awareness survey of four Western countries, not a framework ([IDC/Cohere InfoBrief](https://go.cohere.com/rs/003-EZL-678/images/IDC-Sovereign-AI-InfoBrief.pdf?version=1)). IDC's telecom blog suggests telcos could become "national AI factories" ([IDC telecom blog](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/from-connectivity-to-intelligence-telcos-at-the-crossroads-of-ai-sovereignty-and-sustainable-growth/)).

### 5.3 Telecom analysts: the only published operator archetypes

## 6. Think tanks and public bodies refuse a single ladder and grade control quality instead

### 6.1 Tony Blair Institute: "Control, Steer, Depend" per layer and four country pathways

"Sovereignty in the Age of AI: Strategic Choices, Structural Dependencies and the Long Game Ahead" (January 2026) defines sovereignty as "fundamentally a question of agency and choice" and frames a trilemma between domestic control, access to frontier capability and policy coherence: "No state can maximise all three simultaneously." (primary) ([TBI report](https://assets.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/4VErupJ2rMqKhEOlgXVZHz/424793511ddca69b0a00d16addd0c66e/2Z9q9tlnGbuQ5nedmEWoJy--112616012026); [TBI annex](https://images.ctfassets.net/75ila1cntaeh/2Atf3gozyxkZd9eTCc69kg/83873c62030f6fe87695f46510b78c33/Sovereignty_in_the_Age_of_AI_-_Annex_-_TBI_-_Jan_2026.pdf)). The earlier benchmarking report had seen this paper only as a search summary; the notes for this report read it in full. Its taxonomy is **"Control: Direct Command Over Strategic Systems"**, **"Steer: Shaping Outcomes Without Full Ownership"** and **"Depend: Managing Reliance on External Actors"**, the last split into "maker dependence" ("active and negotiated") and "taker dependence" ("passive, with systems adopted wholesale"). It insists that "Control, Steer and Depend are not rungs on a ladder" and that "control is not synonymous with nationalisation or autarky." The unit is a posture per layer of a seven-layer stack (compute infrastructure, energy, data, models, applications, talent and skills, governance), assigned by qualitative judgement in six case studies.

Its four pathways are the nearest think-tank equivalent to country archetypes: "advanced economies such as Japan and France assert sovereignty through fallback capacity and governance credibility"; "capital-rich countries such as the UAE pursue sovereignty through state-led investment and ownership"; "large developing economies such as India and Brazil exercise sovereignty through steering power"; and "developing digital economies such as Kenya advance sovereignty through negotiated dependence". Case labels are "frontier acceleration in the UAE", "regulatory power in France", "digital-public-infrastructure-led scaling in India" and "state-guided co-creation in Brazil". The UAE's layer postures are instructive for the champion crosswalk: Energy, Data and Models are "Control", but **Compute is only "Steer"**, because "reliance on foreign accelerators and hyperscale platforms keeps ultimate control externally anchored". Saudi Arabia is named only as energy-advantaged; Morocco, Turkey, Germany, Korea and Singapore are not case studies. Its seven levers begin with "Secure access to frontier AI models and compute" and "Accelerate AI adoption and diffusion across sectors", and the foreword advises that "Leaders should therefore resist the urge to own every model and data centre".

### 6.2 Carnegie: scenarios, six middle-power types and a sceptical reading of the Gulf

Carnegie's "How Middle Powers Can Survive the AI Race" (28 October 2026) classifies futures rather than countries, on "the speed of frontier progress" and "the 'frontier premium,' the gap between the best systems and open-source or other cheap alternatives" (primary) ([Carnegie, October 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/10/ai-frontier-technology-middle-power-countries-four-scenarios)). The four scenarios are "Chokepoint World", "Transform or Die", "Adopt for Advantage" and "Tiered Access". Its conclusion ranks strategies by robustness: "finding sources of leverage at supply chain chokepoints is valuable in almost every technological future"; adoption capacity pays off in most; and sovereign frontier models pay off only in "Adopt for Advantage" — "investments in leverage and adoption pay off in more scenarios than investments in sovereign AI". It names six middle-power types: "Semiconductor chokepoint powers, energy-rich compute hosts, physical and industrial powers, talent- and software-rich fast followers, regulated-sector incumbents, and AI-exposed services exporters." Examples in the text place Germany and South Korea among chokepoint holders; France, India and the UAE among compute hosts; Singapore among fast followers. Saudi Arabia, Morocco, Brazil and Turkey are not named. A frontier-equivalent sovereign model "could cost as much as $900 billion".

Three other Carnegie pieces bear on champions. "Early Lessons in the Pursuit of Sovereign AI" (17 June 2026) compares India and the UAE without assigning archetype labels; it describes G42 as "a private AI company chaired by the UAE's national security adviser", judges that the UAE's sovereign ambitions "have yet to fully take off", and reports that analysts suggest India's national and sector-specific model approach "may be more financially feasible for Saudi Arabia over the next decade" ([Carnegie, June 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/06/early-lessons-in-the-pursuit-of-sovereign-ai)). "The Compute Coalition" (June 2026) models a 100 MW AI data centre in ten countries and finds "The United States and the United Arab Emirates (UAE) top our country competitiveness rankings, in large part because major projects move fastest in those countries", while warning that "A heavy concentration in the Gulf would bring additional geopolitical risks the market may not price in" ([Carnegie Compute Coalition](https://assets.carnegieendowment.org/files/Phillips-Robins%20et%20al._Compute_final.pdf)). Luca Belli's "Operationalizing AI Sovereignty Through Agency, Interoperability, and Openness" (3 September 2026) offers three tests for any "sovereign" initiative — whether it increases independent choice, reduces lock-in and preserves openness — and flags "localized cloud infrastructure that depends on foreign providers" as a warning sign ([Carnegie/Belli](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/09/operationalizing-ai-sovereignty-through-agency-interoperability-and-openness)).

### 6.3 Other think tanks: leverage, pathways and a project census

The German Marshall Fund's "Middle Powers' Bounded Ambition in AI" (22 October 2026) defines an AI middle power by strategic assets (material; industrial and knowledge; institutional and market) and leverage types (diversification, denial, conditioning, bargaining), and treats G42 and HUMAIN as bargaining cases; it records that HUMAIN "received approval for advanced NVIDIA chips, with security and reporting conditions" and that Türkiye "Hosts Huawei Cloud and a planned Google sovereign cloud region" ([GMF](https://gmfus.org/news/middle-powers-bounded-ambition-ai)). Chatham House's "How middle powers can weather US and Chinese AI dominance" (16 February 2026) reportedly sets out four pathways — specialize, align, share, hedge — but the page was blocked and this is a search summary only ([Chatham House](https://chathamhouse.org/2026/02/how-middle-powers-can-weather-us-and-chinese-ai-dominance)). Brookings holds that "full-stack AI sovereignty is structurally infeasible for almost any country" and that AI "is not a sector to build; it's a capability to deploy" ([Brookings, February 2026](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/is-ai-sovereignty-possible/); [Brookings, March 2026](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-national-ai-plans-get-wrong-and-how-to-fix-them)). The CNAS "Sovereign AI Index" (updated 18 August 2026) counts 184 projects, 59% infrastructure, 32% models and 9% data; NVIDIA supplies GPUs for 45% of tracked infrastructure projects (Carnegie's June essay cites 52% from an earlier data cut), and Meta's Llama is the base for 14 of 25 model projects that disclose one ([CNAS](https://interactives.cnas.org/?p=2715)). IISS's "Gulf AI infrastructure and the limits of technological sovereignty" (8 June 2026) reportedly finds that Gulf investment has created jurisdictional, technological and security dependencies on the US (search summary; PDF blocked) ([IISS](https://www.iiss.org/globalassets/media-library---content--migration/files/publications/strategic-comments-delta/2026/06/iiss_gulf-ai-infrastructure-and-the-limits-of-technological-sovereignty_08062026.pdf)).

### 6.4 Stanford HAI: the closest thing to a typology of commercial sovereignty offerings

The Stanford HAI issue brief "The Commercial Landscape of AI Sovereignty Offerings" (July 2026) classifies what vendors sell (primary) ([Stanford HAI brief](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai-issue-brief-the-commercial-landscape-of-ai-sovereignty-offerings.pdf); [HAI news article](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/the-ai-sovereignty-paradox-should-countries-buy-build-or-lease-to-maintain-strategic-control-of-their-ai)). Its three hyperscaler tiers are **"Tier 1. Hyperscaler-operated clouds with technical 'sovereignty controls'"**, **"Tier 2. Partner-operated clouds"** (a local entity operates hyperscaler technology, "effectively creating a jurisdictional 'shield'", with S3NS and Bleu as examples) and **"Tier 3. Disconnected and air-gapped clouds"**. Beyond the hyperscalers it identifies Nvidia "AI factories" (25 countries as of June 2026), through which "Nvidia vows to transform national telecom providers into 'sovereign infrastructure operators'"; OpenAI for Countries (18 initiatives), which "sells access to AI capabilities rather than ownership"; and smaller firms' "'Full-Stack' Sovereignty Platforms" versus "Stack Layer-Specific Sovereignty Offerings". The accompanying article's title poses "Buy, Build, or Lease", but neither document defines those as formal categories. On state-backed companies it observes that "Many of the most mature and advanced companies are actively backed by their governments… with governments directing funding, structuring procurement, and, in some cases, selecting specific companies to build out domestic AI capacity on their behalf", citing Sarvam in India and the five teams Korea selected, and adds that "All of these companies maintain notable foreign dependencies". Its verdict on the commercial pitch is blunt: "They promise that countries will own their AI stack, but at the same time they deepen dependencies on U.S. Big Tech."

### 6.5 Compute geography and readiness indices: capability groupings, not strategy archetypes

The Oxford Internet Institute paper "Compute North vs. Compute South" (2024) divides the world into "'Compute North' countries that host AI compute relevant for AI development (ie. training), 'Compute South' countries whose AI compute is more relevant for AI deployment (ie. running inferencing), and 'Compute Desert' countries that host no public cloud AI compute at all" (primary) ([Oxford paper](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:6306c118-58ca-49ba-b7a0-4ee7b9423d5a/files/ssx61dp632); [AAAI proceedings](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/31683)). The data are a census of six hyperscalers' cloud regions as of October 2023. Applying the paper's rule to its table (derived) puts France, Germany, South Korea, Singapore and India in Compute North; Saudi Arabia, Brazil and Turkey in Compute South; and the UAE, Qatar and Morocco in the Compute Desert. That result is dated and public-cloud-only; Gulf builds since 2024 by non-hyperscalers are outside its scope by construction.

The index-based groupings measure general readiness. UNCTAD's Technology and Innovation Report 2025 uses four categories — "Leaders", "Creators", "Practitioners" and "Laggards" — on proxy indicators for infrastructure, data and skills, and a frontier technologies readiness index of 170 economies ([UNCTAD](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/tir2025_en.pdf)). The World Bank's Digital Progress and Trends Report 2025 groups countries as low, medium or high readiness, with capability stages "Adopt", "Adopt and adapt" and "Adopt, adapt, and innovate", and prescribes by tier: low — "Rely mostly on cloud computing and foreign data centers"; medium — "Invest in domestic data centers", "Provide data embassy and regional data centers for small countries", "Partner strategically with foreign cloud and AI chip providers"; high — "Build AI data centers" ([World Bank](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/d2ac1ea9-b70e-4080-b5de-8b31098e992f/content)). The IMF's AI Preparedness Index (search summary) and Oxford Insights' Government AI Readiness Index 2025 complete the set ([IMF AIPI](https://imf.org/external/datamapper/datasets/AIPI); [Oxford Insights](https://oxfordinsights.com/ai-readiness/government-ai-readiness-index-2025/)). The OECD's compute blueprint (2023) uses three dimensions — capacity, effectiveness, resilience — and no country typology (search summary) ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/a-blueprint-for-building-national-compute-capacity-for-artificial-intelligence_876367e3-en.html)). The Ada Lovelace Institute's mapping of "public compute" finds "a landscape characterised by pluralism and experimentalism" and warns of "value capture", "the risk of public investment primarily benefiting private interests" ([Ada Lovelace Institute](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Policy-briefing-Mapping-global-approaches-to-public-compute.pdf)).

### 6.6 Public grading schemes: the EU SEAL levels and national qualifications

The European Commission's Cloud Sovereignty Framework (version 1.2.1, October 2025) is the only scheme with published weights (primary) ([EC Cloud Sovereignty Framework](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/09579818-64a6-4dd5-9577-446ab6219113_en?filename=Cloud-Sovereignty-Framework.pdf)). It scores eight objectives: Strategic Sovereignty 15%; Legal & Jurisdictional 10%; Data & AI 10%; Operational 15%; Supply Chain 20%; Technology 15%; Security & Compliance 10%; Environmental Sustainability 5%. The assurance levels are: **"SEAL-0 No Sovereignty"**; **"SEAL-1 Jurisdictional Sovereignty: EU law formally applies with limited practical enforceability"**; **"SEAL-2 Data Sovereignty: EU law applicable and enforceable, with material non-EU dependencies remaining"**; **"SEAL-3 Digital Resilience: EU law applicable and enforceable, EU actors exercising meaningful but not full influence"**; and **"SEAL-4 Full Digital Sovereignty: Technology and operations under complete EU control, subject only to EU law, with no critical non-EU dependencies."** SEAL is a floor — "Tenders that do not offer the required (minimum) levels of assurance consistently across all objectives will be rejected" — and the weighted score is an award criterion. A Commission explainer of 1 June 2026 describes SEAL-3 as technological autonomy, a slightly different label from the framework's "Digital Resilience" ([EC explainer](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/sovereign-cloud-framework-explained-2026-06-01_en)). The earlier benchmarking report recorded the Commission's own statement that SEAL-4 "is not today relevant" given chip dependence ([EC lessons learnt, as cited in the earlier report](https://commission.europa.eu/document/download/2ad80a48-166f-4c77-a513-80c53ca2a128_en)).

The first tender (17 April 2026; up to EUR 180 million over six years) is the only case in this literature where a scheme was applied to named providers with a public result. A Post Telecom-led partnership with OVHcloud and CleverCloud, STACKIT, and Scaleway reached SEAL-3; a Proximus-led partnership using S3NS (Thales with Google Cloud technology) reached SEAL-2, the minimum; the Commission commented that "even non-European technologies, when operated within a strict and appropriate framework, can meet the minimum level of sovereignty required." ([EC tender result](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/commission-advances-cloud-sovereignty-through-strategic-procurement-2026-04-17_en)). CISPE's Secretary General reportedly called the outcome "an own goal that threatens to institutionalize sovereignty washing at the highest levels" (search summary) ([Raconteur](https://www.raconteur.net/data-sovereignty/data-sovereignty-washing-is-an-increasing-risk-europe-cant-afford-to-ignore)). National schemes rest on secondary sources in the notes: France's SecNumCloud qualification with its cap on non-EU shareholding; Germany's C5 attestation and the BSI's "C3A" sovereignty criteria of April 2026; and the EU's candidate EUCS scheme with 'basic', 'substantial' and 'high' levels ([cloudmagazin on C3A](https://www.cloudmagazin.com/en/2026/04/27/bsi-cloud-sovereignty-six-dimensions/); [ENISA EUCS](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/publications/EUCS%20%E2%80%93%20Cloud%20Service%20candidate%20cybersecurity%20certification%20scheme.pdf)).

### 6.7 Builders and critics

Two public documents argue for building. EuroStack (Bertelsmann Stiftung, February 2025) proposes a seven-layer European stack and "€300 billion over ten years", warning that otherwise "Europe risks becoming a 'digital colony'"; the researchers did not find the "three scenarios" structure sometimes attributed to it ([EuroStack](https://www.bertelsmann-stiftung.de/fileadmin/files/user_upload/EuroStack__2025_final.pdf)). The UK Compute Roadmap (July 2025) states "we do not define sovereignty as isolation or self-sufficiency", but wants capacity "physically located in the UK… not just capacity rented from abroad" ([UK Compute Roadmap](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/68791dc82bad77c3dae4dddb/uk_compute_roadmap_print-version.pdf)). Critics go the other way. A review of 775 non-US data centres finds US companies operate about 48% of projects by investment value: "building data centers locally does not guarantee digital sovereignty if those facilities are run by foreign entities" ([arXiv 2508.00932](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00932)). Tech Policy Press argues that "The pursuit of national sovereignty undermines the civic sovereignty of individuals" ([Tech Policy Press](https://techpolicy.press/how-national-ai-clouds-undermine-democracy)). The AI Now Institute's "AI Nationalism(s)" collection, the "Sovereignty Theater" paper and the African Union Continental AI Strategy were seen only as search summaries and contain no typology in what was retrieved ([AI Now](https://ainowinstitute.org/ai-nationalisms); [AAAI, Sovereignty Theater](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/43200); [African Union](https://au.int/sites/default/files/documents/44004-doc-EN-_Continental_AI_Strategy_July_2024.pdf)).

## 8. Consolidated view: the frameworks agree on substance and differ on what they are classifying

### 8.1 Where the frameworks converge

Five positions are shared across all four source groups. **Full-stack sovereignty is not available** to anyone outside the United States and China: WEF/Bain ("Full AI sovereignty is not feasible for economies"), BCG ("owning all four is exceedingly rare"), Strategy& (the winners "will not be those that attempt to own the entire AI value chain"), Deloitte ("total sovereignty is unlikely to be achieved by any country or region"), Brookings ("structurally infeasible for almost any country") and CNAS ("it is hard to envision any sovereign compute project outside of China wholly independent of the U.S. technology stack") say the same thing in different words. **Sovereignty is a spectrum assessed layer by layer**, not a status: this is the logic of TBI's postures, Strategy&'s matrix, IDC's attributes, Capgemini's levels and the EU's SEAL scale. **Partnership is the default prescription**, with control reserved for data, identity, critical workloads, physical sites and sector applications, and with cloud platforms and frontier models partnered. **Adoption matters at least as much as building**: TBI's second lever, Carnegie's robustness ranking, Brookings' "a capability to deploy", BCG Henderson's "adoption pull" and Accenture's advice to concentrate "sovereign investment at the application layer" all point the same way. **Local hosting is not control**: the 775-data-centre review, Stanford HAI and the EU tender result agree that where a system sits says little about who commands it.

There is also convergence on the shape of the control continuum. Independent sources arrive at roughly the same six waypoints, which Table 5 aligns. The alignment is the researchers' and this report's mapping; no publisher states it.

### 8.2 Where they diverge

The frameworks disagree on four things, and each disagreement bears on champion design.

**Whether building sovereign infrastructure is worthwhile.** Carnegie, Brookings, TBI and BCG Henderson are sceptical: Brookings warns that "sovereign AI systems may lead to stranded or underused investment". EuroStack, the UK roadmap, the Oxford compute-geography authors and the World Bank (from medium readiness upward) treat territorially controlled infrastructure as valuable in itself. WEF Pathway 4 goes further and recommends that capital- and energy-rich economies "invest in excess AI infrastructure capacity". A government can find a reputable framework to support either choice.

**Whether partner-operated hyperscaler technology is sovereign.** IDC rates "Sovereign partnership" higher on technical sovereignty than a domestic cloud; Lünendonk's buyers rate it the most relevant model for critical workloads; the EU admitted it at SEAL-2; european.cloud and CISPE deny it the label; Stanford HAI calls it a "jurisdictional 'shield'". The dispute is about which attribute counts: IDC and buyers weigh capability and exit clauses, european.cloud and CISPE weigh software ownership.

**How to read the Gulf.** TBI (January 2026) describes the UAE as "turning external reliance into a managed asset rather a vulnerability". Carnegie (June 2026) says its ambitions "have yet to fully take off"; IISS (June 2026, search summary) stresses dependence on the US; Carnegie's siting model ranks the UAE first with the United States but drops it to fourth with an eighteen-month war-related delay. The notes observe that frameworks published after mid-2026 are noticeably more cautious about Gulf concentration and vendor-led sovereignty than those of January 2026.

**What an archetype is.** WEF counts value-chain elements covered. McKinsey asks whether a nation produces for home or for export. BCG measures readiness. IDC's country scheme reads posture and governance. TBI refuses a ladder altogether. Carnegie classifies sources of leverage. The same country therefore receives labels that cannot be reconciled because they answer different questions.

**Provider and operator archetypes.** The published provider classes are the hyperscaler; the national champion or "Frontliner", typically a telecom; the neocloud; the federated consortium; the sovereign model provider; and the integrator (Accenture, EY). Investors are treated as a separate role: McKinsey's "Strategic champion investor" for private equity and sovereign funds, and Strategy&'s instruction that "Sovereign wealth funds will need to secure access and wield influence." For telecom operators specifically there are three overlapping schemes: STL's routes (own cloud; hyperscaler partnership; federated edge), Twimbit's four archetypes (appliance; national-scale infrastructure; orchestration; distributed fabric) and EY's two roles (enabler; beneficiary). They classify by where in the stack the operator takes control, whereas the cloud frameworks classify by legal and operational control; an operator therefore needs a label on each axis.

**Deployment and control-level archetypes.** Table 5 gives the consolidated continuum. Demand evidence favours the middle of it: partnership models tie for second in IDC's adoption data at 35%, sole reliance on a local provider accounts for 5% of the most sensitive data, and buyers accept a premium of roughly 10–15% but not capability loss.

**Grading scales.** Two kinds exist and should never be merged. Assurance scales grade a service: the EU's SEAL-0 to SEAL-4 with eight weighted objectives, SecNumCloud, C5 with C3A, the candidate EUCS levels, IDC's high/moderate/limited ratings per attribute, and STL's ladder from "Storage sovereignty" to "Technological sovereignty". Readiness scales grade a country: BCG's ASPIRE, UNCTAD's index, the World Bank's tiers, the IMF index and Oxford Insights. The first kind measures control; the second measures general capacity to adopt.

### 8.4 What none of the frameworks covers

Seven gaps are common to the whole literature as recorded in the notes. **No framework classifies national champions as companies** by ownership, mandate and governance; the notes record that no peer-reviewed typology of AI national champions or Gulf state-capital models was found and that no consultancy gives detailed guidance on ownership structures. **No framework measures delivery against announcement**: placements rest on strategies, flags and project counts. **No framework assesses commercial sustainability**, meaning revenue outside government and related parties. **Export-control and licensing contingency is discussed but is not a classification variable** anywhere. **The relationship between several state-owned vehicles in one country** — the HUMAIN and stc question — is not addressed. **Feature lag and cost of partner-operated clouds are not quantified** beyond willingness-to-pay surveys. **Coverage of North Africa is absent**: Morocco is unplaced in every strategy typology, and stc, e&, Core42 and Turkcell are not placed by name in any provider framework the researchers could open. Two further silences are worth naming: no telecom framework considers an incumbent operator under foreign majority control, and no framework has an exit or failure category.

The central finding is structural. The published frameworks keep two questions apart: who the provider is, and what deployment model it runs. The coordinator's list answers the first for C1, C2 and C4 and the second for C3, C5 and C6. The earlier benchmarking evidence shows why this matters. Deutsche Telekom appears in STL's material with three separate plays at once, an own-stack cloud, an open-source cloud and a Google-based sovereign cloud, so it is a C2 owner running C3 and C5 deployments simultaneously ([STL/Red Hat deck](https://stlpartners.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/presentations/2026-06-16+STL+Partners+&+Red+Hat+Telco+Sovereignty+webinar+presentation.pdf)). stc delivers sovereign cloud through Oracle Alloy hosted in center3 sites, which is a C2 owner running a C5 deployment ([Saudi Press Agency](https://www.spa.gov.sa/en/N2317972)). Turkcell, which told investors its Ankara hyperscale capacity will be "fully allocated to Google", is a C2 owner acting as host only ([Turkcell Q3 2026 call](https://www.earningswhispers.com/transcript/TKC/Q32026)). A single-axis list cannot hold these cases without forcing them.

The published frameworks also test the archetypes' relative merit, and the result is uncomfortable for the own-stack case. IDC rates "Domestic public cloud" low on technical sovereignty and ecosystem access; Lünendonk's respondents see European providers as far less mature in higher-value services; Forrester places OVHcloud as a Contender; and yet the EU graded own-stack European providers one level above the partner-operated offer. The frameworks therefore corroborate C3 as the higher-assurance but lower-capability choice and C5 as the reverse. The earlier report's finding that Bleu was still unqualified under SecNumCloud in October 2026 is a reminder that the capability advantage of C5 does not guarantee the assurance outcome either ([Next.ink](https://next.ink/260765/secnumcloud-bleu-vise-desormais-une-qualification-pour-la-fin-dannee/)).

Two published provider classes have no counterpart in the coordinator's list. Accenture's **"Federated consortia"** — and the telecom-led consortia that won two of the four EU contracts, with a third going to a telecom group's cloud subsidiary — describe a champion that is an alliance, not a company. And the neutral colocation host, which IDC's archetype 8 treats as the venue with the highest assured control sold directly, is a role the benchmarking found in practice (Khazna in the UAE; N+ONE hosting Oracle's region in Morocco) but did not name as an archetype.

Accenture names "Presight AI and Mozn" at the application layer, Strategy& wants national champions to "create data and use cases", and BCG Henderson's "adoption pull" is one of four resilience components. The earlier report treated Presight inside G42; the frameworks suggest the application layer deserves its own line.

## 10. What we can learn from the frameworks

### 10.1 On method: what makes an archetype scheme useful

**Fix one unit of analysis and say so.** The schemes that confuse readers are the ones that slide between country, provider and deployment. McKinsey's deck is clear precisely because it prints four separate schemes for four units. A champion scheme should classify companies and consortia, and refer out to a country scheme and a deployment scheme.

**Use more than one axis.** TBI's insistence that its postures "are not rungs on a ladder" and IDC's eight-attribute matrix both show that a single ordering hides the trade-off that matters. For champions the minimum is an ownership-and-mandate axis and a deployment-and-control axis.

**Publish the rule.** WEF's count of value-chain elements, BCG's weighted index and the EU's floor-plus-score design can each be argued with because the rule is stated. McKinsey's "e.g." lists and Deloitte's descriptive groups cannot. The EU design is the best model: a minimum level that must be met on every objective, plus a weighted score for comparison.

**Make criteria testable.** IDC's eight questions to any provider ("Which entity holds the contract, where is it incorporated… and what happens if its parent is directed to act?"), Arthur D. Little's withdrawal test and Belli's three tests can be applied by a procurement officer. The earlier report's finding that Brazil and Korea make champion status contestable through tests points the same way.

**Classify what is delivered.** No framework does this, and it is the largest methodological weakness in the literature. A scheme for champions should carry, for each case, the status of capacity and revenue (operating, contracted, announced), as the earlier report did.

**Date the labels and expect them to move.** South Korea changed BCG tier in two years; India is described by WEF as mid-transition; the Oxford compute categories rest on October 2023 data. Carnegie's scenario method adds a further discipline: ask in which futures an archetype's investments pay off.

### 10.2 On substance: what national champions should and should not do

The frameworks agree on what champions should not do. They should not try to replicate hyperscale platforms (Strategy&: "Refrain from competing with hyperscalers across the full platform stack"; BCG Henderson: "The objective is not to catch up with hyperscalers"), should not pursue chip self-sufficiency, and should not build frontier models as a first priority (Carnegie: least robust across scenarios; Strategy&: "a measured approach"). They should not invest ahead of demand: McKinsey's reported main failure mode is mis-sequencing, and Twimbit warns that "An AI factory can be strategically important and still be a questionable investment".

They agree on what champions should do. Own and operate in-country data centres, power and hardened sites; hold critical data, identity and sensitive workloads; operate partnered cloud platforms with local governance and exit rights; adapt and host models, with local-language and sector-specific models as the realistic ambition; and build government and sector applications. The IDC and Lünendonk data add a commercial constraint the strategy houses omit: buyers will pay about 10–15% more and will not accept fewer capabilities, so a champion cannot fund a feature gap through price.

The roles assigned to each actor are consistent across sources. **Governments** set rules, aggregate demand and act as anchor customers (Strategy&; TBI's third lever; WEF's "Aggregate government demand across ministries"); they are orchestrators far more often than owners, and no consultancy recommends a government-owned cloud as the default. **Sovereign wealth funds** secure access and influence and act as champion investors (Strategy&; McKinsey); the think tanks add that this buys speed and "frontier adjacency", not independence, and Carnegie calls the UAE's commitments "geopolitical insurance". **Telecom operators** are the consultancies' and vendors' preferred local anchor (Accenture, IBM, EY, STL), but the telecom analysts who sell to operators are the most sceptical about the economics, forecasting an operator share of GPU-as-a-service of roughly 7% in 2030 (this report's arithmetic on Analysys Mason's figures), and Stanford HAI reads the telecom-as-sovereign-operator model as a vendor go-to-market strategy that relocates dependence. **Private firms** supply product velocity and are where model-layer champions actually come from; Stanford HAI notes that governments increasingly select and fund them, and that all "maintain notable foreign dependencies".

### 10.3 On bias: who paid, who benefits, and what is being measured

**Sponsorship shapes the novel element of each framework.** IDC's three added attributes match Equinix's products; IDC's sovereign-AI awareness survey was commissioned by Cohere; Lünendonk's partners are integrators; STL's deck was produced with Red Hat and includes a Red Hat section on open source; Roland Berger wrote with Aleph Alpha; EY's fourth "actor" is EY's own role; Accenture is delivery partner to the providers it showcases; the WEF paper's contributors include hyperscalers, a neocloud and Gulf investors. The notes record no outside sponsor for the Strategy&, Deloitte APAC and Capgemini reports but also that undisclosed relationships cannot be ruled out.

**The shared conclusion serves the authors.** "Partner, do not build everything" is good advice on the evidence, and it is also the conclusion that maximises work for integrators, advisers and hyperscaler alliance practices. The useful check is to look for sources arguing against their own audience: telecom analysts cautioning operators, BCG Henderson calling stack sovereignty an "illusion" while its parent publishes a sovereignty frame, and Stanford HAI's academic critique. These are the most credible voices in the set.

**Readiness is routinely mistaken for strategy.** BCG's matrix puts Saudi Arabia and the UAE in the same "Rising contender" cell as Brazil and Türkiye. Oxford Insights ranks Saudi Arabia 15th with a compute-capacity score of 25.94. UNCTAD places Morocco in the "Leaders" quadrant on infrastructure proxies that are internet penetration and telecom investment. None of these says anything about who owns or controls infrastructure. A report that merges a readiness rank with a strategy archetype will misplace every Gulf state.

**Self-description is not classification.** Hyperscaler tiers define sovereignty by controls the vendor can deliver and omit software ownership; the Accenture country index is an average of self-assessments by organisations pre-selected for caring about sovereignty.

### 10.4 Implications for Saudi Arabia

**Where the frameworks place it.** McKinsey puts the Kingdom in B2 "AI hubs – producers for export use" with Qatar, the UAE and Singapore. IDC makes it a "Regional Dynamo" with India, Türkiye and Russia. BCG classed it a "Rising contender" in 2024 and in 2026 describes it as pairing "a national AI champion with global chip and cloud partnerships". BCG Henderson lists it with the UAE as a "GenAI middle power" on the strength of centralised capital and energy costs 30–50% below the US, and names Aramco as the champion. STL (probable pairing) classes its sovereignty driver as "Regulatory mandate". WEF does not assign it an archetype. Among institutions it is a GMF bargaining case, a 2023 "Compute South" country on Oxford's rule (derived), and the 15th-ranked government on Oxford Insights' index (derived rank). TBI and Carnegie's October paper do not classify it.

**The pathway the frameworks imply.** By this report's application of the WEF logic, Saudi Arabia sits on Pathway 4 or 5: excess infrastructure capacity serving neighbouring markets, "trusted technology sourcing… and long-term offtake arrangements", hosted external foundation models, PPP incentives and regulatory certainty to "secure foreign direct investment". The WEF's May 2026 description of the draft Global AI Hub Law is the legal instrument for that role. Strategy& supplies the layer-by-layer version: assure chips through allocation and diversification; control physical infrastructure, critical data and critical-sector systems through national champions; partner on cloud platforms and frontier models; and use sovereign capital to influence. McKinsey's reported sequence — prioritised sovereign workloads and lighthouse use cases, then shared infrastructure, then exportable specialisation — and Carnegie's suggestion that national and sector-specific models "may be more financially feasible for Saudi Arabia over the next decade" complete the picture. Accenture's regional finding that 62% of executives see local sovereign AI solutions as "behind" or "not competitive" identifies where the gap is felt.

**Where the frameworks are silent or likely wrong.** Five points stand out against the earlier benchmarking evidence. First, McKinsey's "producers for export use" describes an intention: the earlier report found 1.1 MW of disclosed live capacity at HUMAIN against a target of about 3 GW by 2030 and no published revenue ([The National, 22 October 2026](https://www.thenationalnews.com/future/technology/2026/10/22/saudi-arabias-humain-expands-nvidia-deal-with-84mw-ai-computing-plan/)). The B2 label is a target archetype, which McKinsey's own decision menu acknowledges by listing it as a design option, and should not be read as a description. Second, the frameworks cannot agree on what HUMAIN is (Section 9.2) and none distinguishes it from G42; the benchmarking shows the two differ in structure, disclosure and chip-access status. Third, no framework addresses the question the earlier report treated as urgent: how two PIF-controlled vehicles, HUMAIN and stc/center3, divide the infrastructure, cloud and AI roles. The published two-axis logic helps here, since stc is a telecom frontliner running a sovereign-partnership deployment and HUMAIN is a state-created AI vehicle, but the frameworks offer no guidance on overlap. Fourth, export-control contingency appears only in the think-tank sources (GMF's note that HUMAIN's chip approval came "with security and reporting conditions"; IISS, by search summary) and is absent from the consultancy archetypes that place Saudi Arabia most favourably. Fifth, physical security enters only through Strategy& and Carnegie, both after the 2026 attacks on Gulf data centres; WEF's Pathway 4 advice to build "excess" capacity does not price it.

One framework finding should be taken as a direct challenge. Strategy&, writing for Gulf governments, says the cloud layer should be partnered and not contested, while IDC's survey finds the Middle East, Türkiye and Africa region reporting the widest unmet need in AI sovereignty offerings and in control of data in transit. The implication, which is this report's reading, is that the defensible Saudi champion role lies in sovereign AI platforms, sovereign connectivity and applications — areas where stc's network and HUMAIN's mandate are assets — more than in another cloud region. The readiness indices support the same sequencing: Saudi Arabia scores 93.21 on governance and 88.62 on public-sector adoption in the Oxford Insights data, and 25.94 on compute capacity.

### 10.5 Implications for Morocco

**Where the frameworks place it.** Almost nowhere. BCG's 2024 matrix classes Morocco a "Gradual practitioner", in the lowest non-emergent readiness tier; its 2026 placement is unknown. McKinsey covers it only through "Africa" in archetype A, "AI Consumers & Challengers in AI". WEF, IDC, STL, TBI, Carnegie, GMF, Stanford HAI and CNAS do not mention it. It appears in indices: UNCTAD rank 67 (upper-middle group), with the observation that "Egypt and Morocco exceed the global averages in both indicators" on infrastructure, "partly due to the submarine cables under the Mediterranean"; Oxford Insights score 43.06 and derived rank 86, with governance at 78.75 and compute capacity at 7.69; and an IMF preparedness rank of 96th of 174 (search summary via a secondary source) ([Credit Libanais summary](https://economics.creditlibanais.com/Article/212267)). On the Oxford compute rule it is a "Compute Desert" (derived). The only provider-level mention is Cassava's planned AI-factory expansion.

**The pathway the frameworks imply.** By this report's application, Morocco fits WEF Pathway 3: "Rather than pursuing full sovereign AI infrastructure, these economies can use PPPs, pay-as-you-go pricing models and concessional finance to expand access", with investment in connectivity, curated data and "partnering on open-source or allied foundation models". The World Bank's medium-readiness prescriptions fit too — "Invest in domestic data centers" and "Partner strategically with foreign cloud and AI chip providers" — though the notes are explicit that Morocco's World Bank tier is an inference. TBI's Kenya case offers the posture: "negotiated dependence… shaping the terms on which external capacity is deployed, rather than passively absorbing it." BCG's advice for practitioners is to boost investment in data centres and "attract FDI in AI"; BCG Henderson's is "minimum viable sovereignty" through capacity that complements hyperscalers; the World Bank and the African Union add regional pooling. This is consistent with the earlier report's recommendation of a regulation-first approach and a small sovereign core.

**Where the frameworks help more than expected.** Two published categories give precedent for the earlier report's conclusion that the most plausible Moroccan champion is "a consortium rather than a company". Accenture's "Federated consortia" is a recognised provider type, and two of the EU's first four sovereign-cloud contracts went to telecom-led partnerships (Post Telecom with OVHcloud and CleverCloud; Proximus with S3NS, Clarence and Mistral) rather than to single companies. The EU framework also shows how a qualification regime can grade offers instead of admitting or excluding them, which is directly relevant to Morocco's two-level cloud qualification decree described in the earlier report ([Le360](https://fr.le360.ma/economie/cloud-souverain-le-maroc-franchit-un-pas-decisif-sur-le-terrain-juridique_Y37PYS2R5RDCXL7HS65VPE76Y4/)). In STL's terms, Morocco's driver is a "Regulatory mandate" (this report's application; STL does not place Morocco).

**Where the frameworks are silent or likely wrong.** First, the consumer label is too coarse. "Africa" as a single class ignores Morocco's connectivity position, its qualification regime and its services-export base; the frameworks have no category for a country that is ahead on regulation and behind on assets. Second, the frameworks that do offer a hub route would mislead if applied literally. WEF Pathways 4 and 5 require "high availability of capital, land and energy", and Carnegie's "energy-rich compute hosts" type assumes deliverable power; the earlier report found roughly 1.4 GW announced in Morocco against about 36 MW with any dated commitment, state investment vehicles acting as co-sponsors and a constrained grid. Energy potential alone does not put Morocco on the Gulf pathway. Third, the telecom frameworks assume a domestically controlled incumbent. The earlier report recorded that Maroc Telecom is 53% owned by the UAE's e& ([TelecomTV](https://www.telecomtv.com/content/digital-platforms-services/maroc-telecom-doubles-down-on-digital-sovereignty-55264/)); no telecom archetype considers that case, although the EU framework's strategic-sovereignty factors on change of control and source of financing show how a grading scheme would treat it. Fourth, the Oxford "Compute Desert" label is dated and method-bound: the census covered six hyperscalers as of October 2023 and did not include Oracle, so Oracle's Casablanca region, opened in April 2026 according to the earlier report, would not change Morocco's category on that method ([Hespress](https://fr.hespress.com/468760-468760.html)). Fifth, no framework addresses the constraint the earlier report identified for serving Europe, the absence of an EU adequacy decision.

## 12. Conclusion

The literature is richer on vocabulary than on evidence. It supplies a settled language for describing what a country chooses at each layer and a well-aligned continuum for describing what a given cloud actually is, and both should be adopted in preference to invented terms: "sovereign partnership", "domestic public cloud", "government cloud", "Frontliners", "federated consortia", Control–Steer–Depend, and the SEAL levels. What it does not supply is any way of telling a champion that exists from one that has been announced, or a champion that earns revenue from one that is sustained by its owner. That is where this study's company-level archetypes and its delivery labels add something the frameworks lack, and the right use of the two together is to name champions in the published vocabulary and to grade them on the evidence.

For the two countries of interest the frameworks point in opposite directions from their defaults. Saudi Arabia is placed generously, in the hub class, on the basis of capital, energy and intent; the discipline it should import from the frameworks is the layer-by-layer posture and the sequencing of demand before capacity, and the discipline it should add is disclosure. Morocco is barely placed at all, which is itself useful: it is free to be classified by what it legislates and enforces. The published precedents closest to its situation are a graded qualification scheme and consortium-based champions, both European, and both achievable without Gulf-scale capital.
